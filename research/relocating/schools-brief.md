# PART 1 — Fair housing compliance for real estate agents discussing schools

## 1. What HUD, NAR, and South Carolina actually say

### 1a. HUD — "Dear Colleague" letter, April 24, 2026 (PRIMARY SOURCE, read in full)

- Source: U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development, Office of the Assistant Secretary for Fair Housing and Equal Opportunity, letter dated April 24, 2026, signed Craig W. Trainor, Assistant Secretary, Fair Housing and Equal Opportunity. 7 pages.
- URL (PDF): https://www.hud.gov/sites/default/files/hudclips/documents/AS-Trainor's-DCL-on-Neighborhood-Crime-Data-and-School-Quality.pdf
- HUD press release: https://www.hud.gov/news/hud-no-26-028 (linked from NAR's FAQ page; see 1b)
- Opened and read: 2026-08-16 (full 7-page PDF read).

Operative language, quoted exactly:

> "Contrary to publicly available materials from industry leaders on steering, real estate agents and brokers do not violate the Fair Housing Act merely by discussing with prospective homebuyers or renters the prevalence of crime or the quality of schools in neighborhoods." (p. 1)

> "As a threshold matter, the Fair Housing Act does not categorically restrict realtor speech on crime rates or school quality." (p. 3)

> "The term 'steering' is nowhere to be found in the Fair Housing Act, but the United States Supreme Court has defined racial 'steering' as 'directing prospective homebuyers interested in equivalent properties to different areas according to their race.'" (p. 4, citing Gladstone Realtors v. Vill. of Bellwood, 441 U.S. 91, 94 (1979))

> "The Department's regulations define 'unlawful steering' as restricting or attempting 'to restrict the choices of a person by word or conduct in connection with seeking, negotiating for, buying or renting a dwelling,' 'because of race, color, religion, sex, handicap, familial status, or national origin.'" (p. 4, citing 24 C.F.R. § 100.70(a))

The letter then lists the examples of prohibited steering from 24 C.F.R. § 100.70(c)(1)-(4), quoted in the letter as:
> "discouraging any person from inspecting, purchasing or renting a dwelling ... because of the race ... of persons in a community, neighborhood, or development";
> "[d]iscouraging the purchase or rental of a dwelling because of race ... by exaggerating drawbacks or failing to inform any person of desirable features of a dwelling or of a community, neighborhood, or development";
> "communicating to any prospective purchaser that he or she would not be comfortable or compatible with existing residents of a community ... because of race"; and
> "assigning any person to a particular section of a community ... because of race." (p. 4)

> "Case law and the Department's own regulations make clear that statements made without the intent to direct a client based on his race or the prevailing racial characteristics of a neighborhood do not constitute unlawful racial steering." (p. 5)

> "As the United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit explained, 'steering implies different treatment of testers [or buyers] of different races. A broker determined to "steer" all customers, of whatever race, to a particular neighborhood is not guilty of racial steering, because he is not treating the races differently.' Likewise, a broker determined to steer all customers—regardless of race—toward low-crime areas with good schools is treating the races equally and not engaging in prohibited steering." (p. 5, citing Vill. of Bellwood v. Dwivedi, 895 F.2d 1521, 1528 (7th Cir. 1990))

> "While it is unlawful for realtors to treat their clients differently based on race or guide their clients with racial language, information about crime rates and school ratings is relevant to all races." (pp. 5-6)

> "FHAPs should not issue findings of discrimination based on real estate professionals providing school and crime data to customers in an equal and consistent manner or truthfully answering nonracial questions on those topics. Similarly, FHIPs should not use federal funds to file fair housing complaints premised on real estate professionals providing school and crime data in an equal and consistent manner or truthfully answering nonracial questions on those topics." (p. 6)

> "The Department strongly urges industry to revisit ethics training materials and reconsider public statements that stifle real estate agent speech related to nonracial neighborhood characteristics of vital importance to Americans deciding on where to live and raise their families." (p. 7)

What the letter is and is not (my reading, for the page team):
- It is an enforcement-posture statement from HUD's FHEO. It says HUD-funded FHAP agencies (SC Human Affairs Commission is one — see 1d) and FHIP grantees should not pursue cases based on agents "providing school and crime data to customers in an equal and consistent manner or truthfully answering nonracial questions."
- It does NOT amend the statute (42 U.S.C. 3604) or the regulation (24 C.F.R. 100.70). Both still prohibit restricting choices "because of" a protected class, including by "exaggerating drawbacks or failing to inform" about a community.
- It is not binding on courts or private plaintiffs. NAR's June 5, 2026 FAQ (1b) says exactly that.
- The letter's own conditions are: information shared "in an equal and consistent manner," "truthfully," and "nonracial." Those are the three conditions our page must satisfy on its face.
- The letter criticizes, by name and URL, three NAR articles (Mar. 31, 2020 "The Gentrification Conversation"; Apr. 2, 2023 "Fair Housing Is In Your Hands"; Jan. 3, 2023 "How to Handle Client Questions About Schools and Neighborhoods") and one NAR piece from Apr. 17, 2024 ("The Safety Series: 'Is This a Safe Neighborhood?' Don't Answer That"). Do not cite those older NAR pieces as current NAR policy without noting NAR's June 5, 2026 FAQ.

### 1b. NAR — "FAQs on Steering, Crime and Schools," June 5, 2026 (PRIMARY SOURCE, all nine accordion answers read)

- URL: https://www.nar.realtor/fair-housing/faqs-on-steering-crime-and-schools
- Date shown on page: June 5, 2026. Opened 2026-08-16 in browser; accordion answers expanded via DOM.

Intro text, verbatim:
> "On Friday, April 24, 2026, the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) issued a 'Dear Colleague' letter stating that it is not a violation of the Fair Housing Act to share information with prospective homebuyers about neighborhood crime rates and school quality, if such information is shared consistently and without discriminatory intent.
>
> The National Association of REALTORS® (NAR) agrees that consumers seeking information about schools and crime should have access to accurate and complete data during their home search. REALTORS® must also be mindful of the Fair Housing Act's prohibition on steering, which prohibits agents from directing buyers towards or away from a neighborhood because of the client's race, national origin, religion, or other protected characteristics. In some cases, courts have held that racially-coded comments about crime and schools can be evidence of discriminatory intent under the Fair Housing Act.
>
> To avoid steering, agents should share the same kind of neighborhood information with all clients, regardless of their background. Remaining objective and factual, and avoiding subjective commentary, personal opinions, or hearsay, can help agents mitigate this risk."

Q&A, verbatim:

**Q: What is steering under the Fair Housing Act?**
> "The Supreme Court in 1982 described racial steering as a 'practice by which real estate brokers and agents preserve and encourage patterns of racial segregation … by steering members of racial and ethnic groups' to neighborhoods occupied primarily by members of those same groups, and away from neighborhoods inhabited by members of other groups.
>
> In its long-standing Fair Housing Act regulations, HUD has defined illegal steering to include discouraging the purchase of a home because of the client's race or other protected characteristic, 'by exaggerating drawbacks or failing to inform any person of desirable features of a dwelling or of a community, neighborhood, or development.'
>
> Courts have found illegal steering in two main types of practices by real estate agents: (1) providing more listings in White neighborhoods to White clients than to Black or Hispanic clients; and (2) providing more negative information about integrated communities to White clients than to minority clients.
>
> In addition, courts have identified that, in some situations, comments about neighborhood crime rates or school quality (comments such as 'high-crime neighborhood' or 'poor schools') can be seen as 'code words' to signify the race or national origin of neighborhood inhabitants. In such cases, 'code words,' just like explicit statements about race, can be evidence of the speaker's intent to discriminate. The mere communication of factual information about neighborhood schools or crime, shared consistently with all clients, should not constitute the unlawful use of code words. Finders of fact in a fair housing case will consider other relevant facts and circumstances to determine if the agent was engaged in racial steering."
> Footnotes cite Havens Realty Corp. v. Coleman, 455 U.S. 363, 366 n.1 (1982); 24 C.F.R. § 100.70(c)(2); Atkins v. Robinson, 545 F. Supp. 852, 874 (E.D. Va. 1982) ("An abundance of crime" … "may be interpreted as [a] veiled reference to race."); McGinest v. GTE Service Corp., 360 F.3d 1103, 1117 (9th Cir. 2004); Galdamez v. Potter, 415 F.3d 1015, 1024 n.6 (9th Cir. 2005); Smith v. Fairview Ridges Hosp., 625 F.3d 1076, 1085 (8th Cir. 2010).

**Q: Is it illegal to talk about neighborhood school and crime data with clients?**
> "No. But agents must communicate the same information to all clients regardless of the client's race, national origin, or other protected characteristics. To mitigate legal risks, agents should make sure their comments about neighborhood characteristics are based on facts, not hearsay or opinion."

**Q: What are the legal risks in offering opinions to clients about schools and crime?**
> "Subjective commentary is not automatic evidence of discriminatory intent. But subjective commentary raises the risk that an agent may present biased information. That doesn't mean that every time an agent shares an opinion, it's discriminatory. But if an agent makes either complimentary or critical comments about neighborhood features because of the race, nationality, or religion of the homebuyer or the demographic characteristics of the neighborhood, the agent could be exposed to charges of discrimination. Keeping statements factual and objective mitigates this risk.
>
> A 2019 hidden-camera investigation in Long Island, NY, conducted by the newspaper Newsday illustrated how agents' subjective comments about crime and schools could become evidence of possible discrimination. In disciplinary proceedings, New York administrative law judges wrote that the personal opinions about local schools that these agents shared constituted a 'proxy' for race, even when agents had not openly discussed race. For example, agents had recommended buyers avoid a majority-minority district where the high school had won numerous accolades, boasted a 97% graduation rate, and where nearly half the students earned advanced Regents Diplomas. These actions demonstrate the legal risks agents face when they steer clients away from schools based on their own subjective opinions, rather than affording the homebuyers the opportunity to make a decision based on the objective data."

**Q: What should I say if a client asks, 'Is this neighborhood safe'?**
> "Ground any response in objective, factual information. While reliable sources of crime data vary by location, you may be able to get statistics from your local police department. You must provide the same information to all clients. You can also refer the client to the local police department's public affairs officer so the client can engage directly about their safety concerns."

**Q: What should I say if a client asks, 'Are there good schools in the area'?**
> "Ask the client what they are looking for in a 'good' school and base your response on reliable, authoritative information. This may include: student-teacher ratios, percentage of students who go on to college, programs for children with special needs, athletics or other extracurriculars, etc. You must provide the same information to all clients. The REALTORS® Property Resource (RPR) and many other websites offer school information. You can also refer the potential homebuyer to the school district's main office or the Parent-Teacher Association (PTA) so they can engage directly."

**Q: Does the April 24, 2026 HUD "Dear Colleague" letter shield agents from liability if they are charged with steering?**
> "Relying solely or exclusively on HUD's letter may not shield agents from liability if they are charged with steering. The letter makes clear agents can provide information to clients about neighborhood schools and crime, when it is shared consistently and without discriminatory intent. Yet, since federal courts have established that comments about crime and schools may constitute evidence of discriminatory intent, there remains a lack of clarity about when sharing such information triggers that legal risk. For this reason, NAR continues to advise that when agents discuss these topics, they provide the same information to everyone and that it be accurate and reliable.
>
> Even if HUD does not bring a case, private litigants and regulators at the state and local level can still allege that an agent's statements about schools and crime are racially coded and evidence of illegal steering under the federal Fair Housing Act and state and local fair housing laws. Courts are not bound by HUD's enforcement posture."

**Q: Does the Fair Housing Act compel agents to share crime and school information?**
> "No."

**Q: Has NAR ever taken the position that agents may never talk to clients about neighborhood schools and crime?**
> "No. Some fair housing trainers or real estate practitioners, whether due to misunderstanding the law, a desire for clear bright-line rules, or in an effort to eliminate all risk, may believe 'it's illegal to talk about it.' Individuals expressing this view may have been quoted in stories on NAR's website. Such quotations, however, are consistently accompanied by NAR's official guidance, as set forth in its Code of Ethics and official training manuals, which frame the issue as one requiring caution and care, not complete avoidance." (footnotes: Code of Ethics Article 10; Standards of Practice 10-1 and 10-2; training manuals from 1985 through 2016)

**Q: Do these FAQs constitute a change in NAR's guidance on discussing schools and crime with clients?**
> "No. NAR's Code of Ethics and Standards of Practice explicitly allow members to share neighborhood demographic information, as long as it is not racial, ethnic, or religious. Since the 1980s, NAR has advised caution about the way schools and crime are discussed, encouraging members to share objective, factual information supported by reliable sources like police departments and school districts (and aggregators of that reliable data).
>
> NAR's guidance on crime data and school quality has been consistent for decades. In light of HUD's letter, however, NAR is underscoring and clarifying certain points and some public misimpressions about our guidance and what the law requires."

### 1c. NAR — "Steering, Schools, and Equal Professional Service," June 9, 2014 (PRIMARY SOURCE, read in full in browser 2026-08-16)

- URL: https://www.nar.realtor/articles/steering-schools-and-equal-professional-service
- Date shown: June 9, 2014. Still live on nar.realtor as of 2026-08-16. NAR's June 2026 FAQ says its guidance "has been consistent for decades"; this is the fullest statement of it that is specific to schools. It is the "Equal Professional Service Model."

Verbatim:
> "'Steering' under the Fair Housing Act is the process of influencing a buyer's choice of communities based upon the buyer's race, color, religion, gender, disability, familial status, or national origin. ... One way such 'directing' can occur is through comments by an agent, either positive or negative, about a community. ... Or if an agent expresses his or her own positive or negative views about certain communities or schools, the purpose of which is to direct a buyer either towards or away from a community, then that agent may be stating a housing preference based on race or familial status or religion."

> "Discussions about schools can raise questions about steering if there is a correlation between the quality of the schools and neighborhood racial composition--or if characterizations such as 'a school with low test scores' or 'a community with declining schools' become code words for racial or other differences in the community. Similarly, making unspoken distinctions by promoting a school in one district while keeping silent about the quality of another school can have the same effect. These become fair housing issues."

> "The keys to the model are offering objective information, providing a variety of choices, and letting the customers set the limits of their housing search."

> "'I often hear REALTORS® say "I'm not allowed to talk about schools,"' says Fred Underwood, director of diversity for the National Association of REALTORS®. 'In fact, schools play an important role in a homebuyer's decision. And it's important for REALTORS® to understand how to address their questions.'"

> "Let objective information, not subjective information, be the guide. When customers ask, 'How are the schools?' The best thing a REALTOR® can do is guide them to third-party information, so they can make a decision on their own. 'It's okay to talk about schools, but don't make the judgment call yourself,' says Underwood. 'Give homebuyers the resources they need to make the decision for themselves. Remember, it's not about your kids. It's about their kids. Every child has different needs, and every family has different preferences.' Objective information is easy to document and quantify and is widely available, given the widespread use of the Internet. Some states even have websites that compare schools. 'Keep a list of school or community-based websites that offer information about schools so you're prepared to provide those web addresses to your customers,' says Underwood. 'Better still, build relationships with local schools, so you know where to direct people's inquiries. Be equipped to provide contact information at schools or make appointments with the schools so your customers can go visit the school and find out for themselves.'"

> "A buyer who wants a home in the Abraham Lincoln school district is providing an objective criterion for a home search. An agent can clearly identify and find housing in that school district. On the other hand, a buyer wanting a home in a 'good school district' is unclear about what he or she wants. Is it the school with the best football team, the most AP students, the newest facility, or something else? Agents attempting to choose homes based on this kind of request substitute their own judgment for the buyer's regarding what makes a school good."

> "A truthful response is not necessarily a violation of the law, but the buyer could perceive such a response, even if truthful, as a way of limiting choices or as discrimination. Instead ask, 'What makes a school good for you?' and then suggest a third-party source such as a school district website."

> "Finally, how does a REALTOR® deal with stark differences in school quality?--By providing access to objective information. 'There's nothing wrong with a REALTOR® providing a list of all the school or community websites or setting up school visits to help buyers get the information they need to make informed decisions,' says Underwood. 'In fact, that's a valuable service, particularly for out-of-towners.'"

Note for the page: the last quote is, almost exactly, the page we intend to build (a list of official school websites and lookup tools for out-of-town buyers). It is NAR's own description of compliant practice.

### 1f. The regulation itself — 24 C.F.R. § 100.70 (read in full via Cornell LII, 2026-08-16)

- URL: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/24/100.70 (eCFR redirected to an anti-bot page; LII mirror used. Citation line on the page: "[54 FR 3283, Jan. 23, 1989, as amended at 78 FR 11481, Feb. 15, 2013; 85 FR 60332, Sept. 24, 2020; 85 FR 64025, Oct. 9, 2020; 88 FR 19500, Mar. 31, 2023]")
> "(a) It shall be unlawful, because of race, color, religion, sex, handicap, familial status, or national origin, to restrict or attempt to restrict the choices of a person by word or conduct in connection with seeking, negotiating for, buying or renting a dwelling so as to perpetuate, or tend to perpetuate, segregated housing patterns, or to discourage or obstruct choices in a community, neighborhood or development."
> "(c) Prohibited actions under paragraph (a) of this section, which are generally referred to as unlawful steering practices, include, but are not limited to:
> (1) Discouraging any person from inspecting, purchasing or renting a dwelling because of race, color, religion, sex, handicap, familial status, or national origin, or because of the race, color, religion, sex, handicap, familial status, or national origin of persons in a community, neighborhood or development.
> (2) Discouraging the purchase or rental of a dwelling because of race, color, religion, sex, handicap, familial status, or national origin, by exaggerating drawbacks or failing to inform any person of desirable features of a dwelling or of a community, neighborhood, or development.
> (3) Communicating to any prospective purchaser that he or she would not be comfortable or compatible with existing residents of a community, neighborhood or development because of race, color, religion, sex, handicap, familial status, or national origin.
> (4) Assigning any person to a particular section of a community, neighborhood or development, or to a particular floor of a building, because of race, color, religion, sex, handicap, familial status, or national origin."

Familial status is a protected class in the same sentence as race. A schools page for "families with children" must therefore also not steer families with children toward or away from any community. Describing a community as "family-friendly" or "great for families" (or the reverse) is a familial-status statement, not just a race proxy. This matters for our page's title and framing.

### 1g. South Carolina — statute, Human Affairs Commission, Real Estate Commission (all read 2026-08-16)

### 1h. Why "school quality" is treated as a proxy for race — the case record (for the page team's understanding, not for the page)

- NAR FAQ (1b) footnote 4 cites Atkins v. Robinson, 545 F. Supp. 852, 874 (E.D. Va. 1982): "An abundance of crime" … "may be interpreted as [a] veiled reference to race."
- Newsday's 2019 "Long Island Divided" investigation, and the New York Department of State disciplinary cases that followed. NAR's FAQ (1b): "New York administrative law judges wrote that the personal opinions about local schools that these agents shared constituted a 'proxy' for race, even when agents had not openly discussed race."
- NY Governor's office press release, Aug. 30, 2022 (https://www.governor.ny.gov/news/governor-hochul-and-attorney-general-james-tackle-discriminatory-practices-long-island-real): an agent "told white homebuyer to avoid school districts where majority of students were of color, saying they were not good schools," and asked "Do you want your kids to be in school with kids that they relate to?"
- Cooley LLP client alert, May 6, 2026 (https://finsights.cooley.com/hud-clarifies-scope-of-fair-housing-acts-steering-prohibition-for-real-estate-professionals/): "state law may impose independent obligations – that is, states may maintain disparate impact standards or impose additional requirements on real estate professionals. ... Practitioners should assess applicable state-level obligations separately from this federal guidance."

Bottom line for item 1: As of 2026-08-16, both HUD (Apr. 24, 2026) and NAR (June 5, 2026) say an agent may share school information. HUD's condition: "in an equal and consistent manner," "truthfully," "nonracial." NAR's condition: "objective and factual," from "reliable, authoritative" sources, "the same information to all clients," "avoiding subjective commentary, personal opinions, or hearsay." NAR warns that HUD's letter does not bind courts, private plaintiffs, or state regulators. A public web page is by definition given to everyone identically, which satisfies the "same information to all clients" condition automatically — the remaining exposure is entirely in wording (subjective characterization, code words, familial-status framing) and in selection (promoting one district's schools "while keeping silent about" another's).

## 2. What IS considered safe practice (sourced)

Every authority I opened converges on the same four practices. Sources are the ones quoted in section 1; only the operative lines are repeated here.

**(a) Give objective facts, not characterizations.**
- NAR FAQ, June 5, 2026: "agents should make sure their comments about neighborhood characteristics are based on facts, not hearsay or opinion." ... "Keeping statements factual and objective mitigates this risk."
- NAR 2014: "Let objective information, not subjective information, be the guide." ... "It's okay to talk about schools, but don't make the judgment call yourself."
- NAR Jan. 3, 2023 (https://www.nar.realtor/magazine/broker-news/network/how-to-handle-client-questions-about-schools-and-neighborhoods, read in browser 2026-08-16): "When talking to clients, it's important to focus the conversation on what the school offers rather than whether it's a 'good' or 'bad' school. Then you can say 'Smith Elementary has this music program, or this program for kids with special needs. That's objective information. It's not your opinion.'" (quoting Alexia Smokler, NAR director of fair housing policy).
- HUD Apr. 24, 2026: information must be shared "truthfully" and be "nonracial."

**(b) Point to official, third-party sources and let the buyer evaluate.**
- NAR 2014: "guide them to third-party information, so they can make a decision on their own." ... "Some states even have websites that compare schools. 'Keep a list of school or community-based websites that offer information about schools so you're prepared to provide those web addresses to your customers.'" ... "'There's nothing wrong with a REALTOR® providing a list of all the school or community websites or setting up school visits to help buyers get the information they need to make informed decisions. In fact, that's a valuable service, particularly for out-of-towners.'"
- NAR FAQ, June 5, 2026: "base your response on reliable, authoritative information. ... You can also refer the potential homebuyer to the school district's main office or the Parent-Teacher Association (PTA) so they can engage directly."
- NAR Jan. 3, 2023: "if clients ask whether ABC Elementary School is a good school, the best response you can provide is to guide them to third-party information, such as the school district's website, so the clients can learn more on their own and come to their own conclusion." ... "'Have your clients get information about local schools directly from the source.'"
- NAR Code of Ethics SOP 10-2: demographic information must be "obtained or derived from a recognized, reliable, independent, and impartial source. The source of such information ... shall be disclosed in reasonable detail."

**(c) Give everyone the same thing.**
- NAR FAQ, June 5, 2026: "agents should share the same kind of neighborhood information with all clients, regardless of their background." ... "You must provide the same information to all clients."
- HUD Apr. 24, 2026: "in an equal and consistent manner."
- NAR Jan. 3, 2023: "brokers can create templates, inserts and printed information to be used with every client. That way, all clients receive the same information ... A broker can create a physical 'source of sources' like a flyer, that provides clients with the various information—school board websites, phone numbers, etc.,—they need to do their own research." (A public web page is the digital form of that flyer.)

**(d) When the buyer uses a vague word, ask what they mean; do not supply your own definition.**
- NAR FAQ, June 5, 2026: "Ask the client what they are looking for in a 'good' school and base your response on reliable, authoritative information. This may include: student-teacher ratios, percentage of students who go on to college, programs for children with special needs, athletics or other extracurriculars, etc."
- NAR 2014: "Instead ask, 'What makes a school good for you?' and then suggest a third-party source such as a school district website."
- NAR Jan. 3, 2023: "When a client uses ambiguous words like 'nice,' 'good,' or 'safe,' ask unbiased questions to better understand what they are looking for."

**Two other practices worth carrying to the page team:**
- Do not promote one district's schools while staying silent about another's (NAR 2014: "making unspoken distinctions by promoting a school in one district while keeping silent about the quality of another school can have the same effect"). For our page: cover both districts (Horry and Georgetown) and every school type (traditional public, magnet/choice, charter, private, home school) with the same depth and the same kind of link.
- Document (NAR 2014: "an agent should keep good records documenting the buyer's initial request, the options provided ..."). For a web page, the analog is a dated sources line and a change log in git.

### 3b. Third-party ratings (GreatSchools, Niche): is linking risky, and what is the recommended alternative

What the authorities say:
- No authority I opened says linking to GreatSchools or Niche is prohibited or, by itself, a violation.
- NAR Jan. 3, 2023: "Many agents opt to point their clients to third-party school rating sites focused on standardized test scores. While this type of information is fine to share with clients, agents should be aware that ratings based on test scores alone may not present full picture of school quality." It adds that NAR's own RPR tool "uses a tool called Niche."
- NAR FAQ, June 5, 2026: "The REALTORS® Property Resource (RPR) and many other websites offer school information."
- HUD Apr. 24, 2026: "information about crime rates and school ratings is relevant to all races." (HUD's letter treats "school ratings" as legitimate information.)
- The contrary caution is from the National Fair Housing Alliance (a plaintiff-side organization) via Chalkbeat, Dec. 5, 2019 (https://www.chalkbeat.org/2019/12/5/21121858/looking-for-a-home-you-ve-seen-greatschools-ratings-here-s-how-they-nudge-families-toward-schools-wi/): Chalkbeat found "The average 1-10 GreatSchools rating for schools with the most low-income and most black and Hispanic students is 4 to 6 points lower than the average score for schools with the fewest black and Hispanic students and fewest low-income students." NFHA general counsel Morgan Williams: "It's a legal gray area. I would discourage real estate service providers from providing information that may be used as a proxy for the racial demographics of a neighborhood." GreatSchools revised its methodology in Sept. 2020 (Chalkbeat, Sept. 24, 2020: https://www.chalkbeat.org/2020/9/24/21453357/greatschools-overhauls-ratings-reduce-link-race-poverty/ — headline only; article not opened).
- HUD's Apr. 2026 letter names NFHA and says following its lead "does not reflect a proper understanding of the Fair Housing Act." NAR's June 2026 FAQ says private litigants can still sue and courts are not bound by HUD.

My assessment for the page team:
1. Linking to a third-party rating site is not, on any source found, a violation. The exposure is reputational and litigation-tactical (a plaintiff could point to a rating we chose to display as a proxy), not a rule.
2. The recommended alternative in every NAR document is the official source: the state report card and the district's own site. NAR 2014: "Some states even have websites that compare schools." South Carolina does: https://screportcards.com (see Part 2, item 7). It is published by the SC Department of Education and the Education Oversight Committee, covers every public school, and lets the reader compare schools and download the data.
3. Important nuance: the official SC report card ITSELF assigns each school an overall rating on a five-word scale (Excellent, Good, Average, Below Average, Unsatisfactory). So "link the official source" does not mean "the buyer sees no rating." It means the rating the buyer sees is the state's, published under state law, with its methodology published, and it is not repeated in our voice.
4. Recommendation: (i) link screportcards.com and the two districts' report-card pages as the primary sources; (ii) do not embed, quote, or restate any school's rating (state or third-party) on our page; (iii) if GreatSchools/Niche are mentioned at all, mention them once, in a "other sites people use" line, with a one-sentence factual note that they are private companies whose scores are calculated from public data and are not the state's report card. Given rule 3 in CLAUDE.md-style caution and the NFHA position, the safest choice is to omit them entirely; the buyer will find them without us.

### 3c. Framing that keeps the page itself compliant

- State plainly on the page that Chapter3 Realty does not rate, rank, or recommend schools, and why (fair housing law prohibits steering; the state publishes the data; the reader decides). This mirrors NAR's advice and is itself protective.
- Present both districts and all school types with the same fields, in a neutral order.
- Every school-related fact carries its source and as-of date (SOP 10-2 standard).
- Tell the reader that attendance zones change and to confirm with the district before making an offer contingent on a school (Part 2, item 4).
- Keep the page's title/H1 neutral: "Schools for buyers relocating to the Myrtle Beach area" (not "Best schools," not "Family-friendly").
- No neighborhood-safety content on this page.
