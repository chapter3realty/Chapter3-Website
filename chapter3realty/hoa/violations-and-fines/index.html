Violations and fines

HOA violations and fines
in South Carolina

WHAT THE LAW SAYS

State law does not mention HOA fines

South Carolina has one law written for homeowners associations. Its core is seven short sections. They cover recording, budget notice, document access and small claims court. Another says this law must not conflict with the state's nonprofit law. None of the seven mention fines.

The words fine and penalty do not appear in it. They do not appear in the state's condo law either. We read both.

The state agency that takes HOA complaints describes the same gap. It says South Carolina has no state law setting out how an HOA must operate. Several other laws may apply.

So there is no legal cap on a fine. There is no legal right to a hearing. There is no set notice period. Whatever rules apply to you sit in your HOA's recorded documents. Nowhere else.

WHERE THE MYTH STARTS

The $100 fine cap comes from a failed bill

Search for HOA fine limits here and you get a tidy set of rules. A $100 cap for each violation. A panel of HOA members that has to hear your side. 14 days written notice before that hearing. None of it is law in South Carolina.

All three come from one bill. Senator Jackson filed the same text three times, in 2009, in 2011 and again in 2014. Each time it went to a subcommittee and went no further.

Each bill would have created a new chapter of the state code. That chapter does not exist. Its page on the state legislature site returns a not found error.

The HOA law that did pass took effect in 2018. It has no fine cap, no hearing step and no panel.

Lawmakers keep trying. Four bills now sit in House committees. One would require 30 days written notice for any fine or debt. It would give a member 30 days to dispute it. One would make every HOA set up its own dispute process, free to use. One would make boards report what they collect in fines each quarter, and where that money goes. One would ban fines for an expired tag on a parked car, boat, trailer or RV. None has passed. Bills move, so check before you rely on any of this.

YOUR OWN DOCUMENTS

The power to fine sits in the recorded documents

The law is silent, so your HOA's power to fine comes from its own papers. The law names the declaration or master deed, the bylaws, and any changes to those. The declaration is the recorded paper that creates the HOA. People call it the covenants, or the CC&Rs. Rules sit outside that list, and they carry their own deadline.

The state does control one thing here, and most owners miss it. With a few exceptions the law lists, these papers must be recorded to be enforced. That happens at the county records office. In Horry County it is the Register of Deeds.

Rules carry a second, harder deadline. A new rule takes effect the moment the board adopts it. But it has to be recorded by January 10 of the next year. If it is not, it stops being enforceable. The same goes for any change to a rule. So a rule can bind you today and lose its force later.

That gives you one thing to do. Find the exact rule you are accused of breaking. Then look it up in the county recording index. The law ties enforcement to recording, so the index is the check. Whether that beats a given fine is a question for a South Carolina attorney. It is worth asking before you pay.

Ask for the papers early. Most HOAs take about three days to hand them over. That is what we watch them do, not a promise we make. Our own step is what we can promise. The papers go into our analysis tool as soon as they arrive. A person reads the output within minutes.

  • ✓The bylaws. How the board runs itself, including any hearing step it chose to give itself.
  • ✓The rules, with the date each was adopted or changed. Check each date against the January 10 that follows.

CONDOS

Condo law gives a lawsuit, not a fine

South Carolina calls a condo a horizontal property regime. A separate state law governs it. Nothing in that law lets a condo board fine anyone. The words fine and penalty never appear in it.

What the law gives instead is a lawsuit. Every owner has to follow the bylaws and the rules adopted under them. The covenants in the master deed bind you too. An owner who does not comply can be sued by the board. The board can sue for money owed for damages. It can also ask a court to order the owner to stop, or do both.

The law gives you the same remedy. An owner who has been wronged can bring that action too. You can sue to make your board follow its own bylaws.

So a fine in a condo comes only from the recorded master deed, bylaws and rules. The remedy the law itself gives is a lawsuit.

IF YOU GET ONE

Where to take a fine you think is wrong

There are two places to take it. Neither is a sure thing.

The first is magistrates court, the state's small claims court. It can hear money disputes arising under the HOA law. The ceiling is $7,500. A claim for a fine or penalty has the same ceiling.

Two limits come with that. A magistrate cannot hear a case that puts title to your property in question. The law spells out one exception. The grant reaches only disputes arising under the HOA law. That law does not deal with fines at all. Whether a fine based on your own papers counts is a question for a South Carolina attorney.

The second route is a complaint to the state consumer agency. Its own report calls the process voluntary mediation. It cannot force an HOA or a homeowner to take part. It cannot require an outcome or arbitrate the dispute. Its legal job is to receive and record the data.

Enforcement is what people complain about most. The agency's latest report covers 2025. It counted 452 HOA complaints raising 1,104 concerns. The biggest single concern was an HOA failing to follow or enforce its own covenants and bylaws. That was 16.8 percent. Our page on South Carolina HOA laws has the resolution figures.

If you are buying, ask two things before you close. Ask whether the seller has an open violation or an unpaid fine. Get the payoff figure in writing. Then ask what the HOA charges at closing. Almost all of them charge something. A few months of dues up front is common. Add a document fee and a fee to open your account. It varies by HOA, so a good agent asks early.

Common questions

Violations and Fines FAQ

Is there a limit on how much an HOA can fine you in South Carolina?

No. The state HOA law contains no cap on fines. The words fine and penalty do not appear in it. They do not appear in the state's condo law either. The $100 cap quoted all over the internet is bill language that never passed. Any limit that applies to you is written into your own HOA's recorded papers and rules.

Does an HOA have to give you notice or a hearing before it fines you?

Not under state law. The HOA law has one notice rule and it is about something else. It requires at least 48 hours notice before the meeting where the board decides to raise the yearly budget. That rule does not reach an HOA set up under the state's nonprofit law. There is no notice period before a fine, no right to a hearing and no panel. Any notice you get comes from your recorded papers.

Can you fight an HOA fine in South Carolina?

There are two places to take it. Neither is a certainty. Magistrates court is the state's small claims court. It can hear money disputes up to $7,500, including a claim for a fine. It cannot hear a case that puts title to your property in question. The law spells out one exception. You can also complain to the state consumer agency, but its process is voluntary mediation. Take a real dispute to a South Carolina attorney.

Can an HOA fine you over a rule that was never recorded?

Recording is what makes a rule enforceable. With a few exceptions the law lists, HOA papers must be recorded to be enforced. That happens at the county records office. Rules have a second deadline. To stay enforceable they must be recorded by January 10 of the year after they were adopted or changed. Look up the rule you are accused of breaking in the county index. Take what you find to a South Carolina attorney.

What happens if you do not pay an HOA fine?

That depends on your recorded papers, and unpaid dues are a separate question. In a condo, the law creates a lien for unpaid shares of common expenses. A lien is a legal claim recorded against the property itself. It ranks ahead of everything except tax liens and mortgages or other liens that are properly recorded. Whether a fine can become a lien depends on your own papers. Read our page on unpaid HOA dues, liens and foreclosure.
