Developer controlled HOAs

Buying into a developer-controlled HOA
South Carolina sets no turnover deadline

By Timmy Fredrick Nash, Broker-in-Charge, 30+ years on the Grand Strand · Reviewed by Devin Day, Operations Officer · Updated July 29, 2026

Turnover is a handover. The developer stops running the HOA and the owners elect their own board. South Carolina has no turnover law. The state's two main HOA laws set no deadline. They set no percentage of homes sold. They set no duty to hand over at all. The recorded documents decide, and the developer wrote them before any owner existed. The 90 and 75 percent figures buyers read about are mortgage rules, not developer duties.

THE DIRECT ANSWER

No South Carolina law forces a turnover

Turnover is the handover. The developer stops running the HOA and the owners elect a board. South Carolina has no law that says when that must happen. Both of the state's main HOA laws are silent on it. Neither sets a percentage of homes sold. Neither sets a deadline. Neither says a developer has to hand over control at all.

So the recorded documents decide. The declaration and the bylaws set the trigger. Both were written by the party that created the community, before a single owner existed. The law calls that party the declarant, and it is normally the developer. The rule was written by the side it applies to.

Many HOAs here are set up as nonprofit corporations. If yours is, a state law hands the developer two tools. It can create classes of members with different voting power. Its votes then count for more than yours. It can also write the bylaws so some board seats are appointed, not elected. Check the recorded declaration and the state's business filings to see which kind yours is.

One piece of state law helps you. Governing documents have to be recorded at the county land records office. If they are not recorded, they cannot be enforced. So the documents are public, and you do not need permission to read them. For more, see our page on South Carolina HOA laws.

THE MONEY BEFORE TURNOVER

The dues you were quoted may be an estimate

Freddie Mac's project rules allow a projected budget when a project has not been turned over yet. So the dues figure you were quoted can be a forecast, not a full year of real bills.

If reserves are underfunded, the bill lands on owners later. A roof or an elevator fails, the reserve account is short, and the money is raised from owners. That is what special assessments are. Freddie Mac is raising the reserve floor it expects, from 10 percent of yearly dues to 15 percent. That starts with loan applications from January 4, 2027. Our page on what HOA fees cover explains where dues go. Our page on reserve funds and reserve studies explains how to read one.

HOAs almost always charge something at closing. A few months of dues up front is common. So is a document fee and an account setup fee. The amounts vary. A good agent asks in advance, so it is not a surprise at closing.

No primary source measures what developers here do with early dues. Check these instead.

  • ✓Budget or projection. Ask whether the dues figure comes from real costs or from an estimate.
  • ✓Reserve study and balance. Ask if a study was done, when, and what is in the account today.
  • ✓Unsold lots. Read what the developer pays on the homes it still owns, and how that duty is worded.
  • ✓Who can raise dues. Read the assessment article for a cap, a limit on yearly rises, and who votes.
  • ✓Notice before a raise. State law requires at least 48 hours notice before the meeting where a budget increase is decided. It does not apply if the HOA is a nonprofit corporation.

THE LOAN

Developer control makes it a new project

Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac buy conventional mortgages, and their rules decide where a lender can lend. Both treat developer control as the dividing line. Fannie Mae calls a project established only when owners control the HOA. At least 90 percent of the units must also have been sold. Other conditions apply too. If the developer still holds the HOA, the project is new. A finished, nearly sold out building can still count as new.

Freddie Mac draws the same line. A project is established when four things are true. It is finished. It has no more phases. At least 75 percent is sold to other buyers. Unit owners run it. Anything else is new, including a project the developer still controls. Freddie Mac dropped its owner occupancy test for established projects only. It still applies where the developer is in control.

Both loan buyers retired their short review. From August 3, 2026, every condo project takes a full review, or a waiver. Developer control still changes your file. A new project's review expires sooner. It has to be finished within 180 days before you sign your note at closing. On an established project it is good for a year. On a long build the review can expire and be run again.

Fannie Mae also wants at least half the units sold or under contract. Those buyers have to plan to live there or use the home as a second home. That covers the project, or the phase your building sits in. Investor contracts do not count. None of this stops you buying. It changes which lenders can work and how long the file takes. If a project cannot pass review, the fallback is a lender outside these rules. Our page on non-warrantable condos covers that. AFTER THE HANDOVER

Turnover is when owners get to decide

A Grand Strand community moved from developer control to an owner-elected board. The exciting ideas came first. A pool. A dog park. A barbecue area. Then the financials were read.

The money went elsewhere. It was spent on storm damage deterrent systems, picked in close work with the insurance underwriters. The next renewal came in under a 5 percent increase. Nearby communities of the same kind saw far larger ones. Every owner was told exactly why. The lawn care contract was rewritten at a better price, to help absorb the insurance cost.

Turnover is where dues can jump. It is also where owners finally get to make the decisions that hold dues down. Which one you get depends on whether the new board reads the numbers.

BEFORE YOU SIGN

Get the documents before you go under contract

So read the numbers first. The state's document rights run to members, and a buyer under contract is not a member yet. Those rights cover the rules, the annual budget and the member list. None of them reach you before closing.

The seller disclosure will not fill the gap. An owner has to say whether the property has an HOA. The same law lets the owner answer that they make no representations. Some sales are exempt from disclosure altogether. The state's 2026 HOA report shows most people who complained did not have the documents before they bought. Our page on which HOA documents to request lists each one and what it shows.

Two routes work. Pull the recorded documents at the county office. Put a document deadline in your contract. Associations usually take about three days to release a package. That is how they behave, not a promise we make. Documents go through our analysis tool right away, and a person reads the output within minutes. Our page on buying new construction on the Grand Strand covers the rest.

Common questions

Developer Control FAQ

When does a developer have to turn over an HOA in South Carolina?

There is no deadline in South Carolina law. Neither of the state's main HOA laws sets a turnover date. Neither sets a percentage of homes sold. Neither creates a duty to hand over control. The recorded declaration and bylaws decide it. Read the declaration and every recorded amendment, and look for what ends developer control. If nothing ends it, that is an answer too.

What percentage of homes has to be sold before HOA turnover in SC?

None. South Carolina sets no percentage that triggers turnover. The percentages people read about come from mortgage rules. Fannie Mae treats a condo project as established only when owners control the HOA. At least 90 percent of units must be sold too. Freddie Mac uses at least 75 percent sold plus owner control. Those are conditions for buying a loan, not duties on a developer.

Can I get a mortgage on a condo when the developer still controls the HOA?

Usually yes, but the review takes longer. Fannie Mae counts any project the developer still controls as new. Both loan buyers retired their short review. From August 3, 2026, every condo project takes a full review or a waiver. A full review on a new project is good for 180 days before you sign your note. On an established project it lasts a year. Ask your lender to start it early.

Will HOA dues go up after the developer turns over the community?

No primary source measures it, so we will not promise a direction. Freddie Mac's rules allow a projected budget when a project has not been turned over. So ask two things. Is the dues figure built from a full year of real costs, and has a reserve study been done? An underfunded reserve is the usual reason a special assessment shows up later.

How do I get the HOA documents before I buy in a new community?

Use the county recording office and your purchase contract. Governing documents have to be recorded to be enforced, so they are public. The state's access rights run to members, and a buyer under contract is not a member yet. They cover rules, the annual budget and the member list. Put a document delivery and review deadline in the contract.
