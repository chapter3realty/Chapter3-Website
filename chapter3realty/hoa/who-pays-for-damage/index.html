Damage and liability

Who pays for water damage
in a condo on the Grand Strand

By Timmy Fredrick Nash, Broker-in-Charge, 30+ years on the Grand Strand · Reviewed by Devin Day, Operations Officer · Updated July 28, 2026

In a South Carolina condominium, two statutes answer this. Section 27-31-240 requires the council of co-owners to insure the property. Section 27-31-250 requires the council to repair insured property that is damaged, and states that the cost of repair or replacement in excess of insurance proceeds and reserve must be considered a common expense. Common expenses are shared pro rata under Section 27-31-190. The chapter never uses the word deductible and never assigns one owner liability to another, so the recorded declaration and the two policies still control the details.

WHAT THE LAW SAYS

What South Carolina law says about paying for condo damage

South Carolina condominiums are governed by the Horizontal Property Act, Title 27 Chapter 31 of the state code. Two sections do the work. Section 27-31-240 is the insurance duty. It says the council of co-owners, meaning the association, shall insure the property against risks, without prejudice to the right of each co-owner to insure his apartment on his own account and for his own benefit. That one sentence is the whole of the duty to buy insurance. It sets no coverage amount, no deductible limit and no list of perils.

Section 27-31-250 is the part most summaries leave out. It says a portion of the property for which insurance is required under Section 27-31-240, and which is damaged or destroyed, must be repaired or replaced promptly by the council of co-owners. There are two exceptions. Repair is illegal under a state statute or local health ordinance, or eighty percent of the co-owners vote not to rebuild. Then subsection (B) answers the money question in one line. The cost of repair or replacement in excess of insurance proceeds and reserve must be considered a common expense.

Read that against Section 27-31-190, which binds co-owners to contribute pro rata toward the expenses of administration and of maintenance and repair of the general common elements and, in the proper case, of the limited common elements. A common expense is a shared expense. So for a covered loss to insured property, the statute points the shortfall at all owners in their percentages. It does not point it at whichever unit the water happened to reach.

There are still real gaps. We read the chapter in full on July 28, 2026. The word deductible does not appear in it anywhere. No section assigns one owner liability for damage to another owner's apartment. And the Act does not spell out how far the insured property reaches into a unit's interior finishes and owner improvements, which is the exact line the declaration draws. That is why the declaration and the two policies still decide the details.

The Homeowners Association Act, Title 27 Chapter 30, adds nothing here. It contains no insurance requirement and no repair rule. Insurance appears once, inside the definition of a homeowners association, which describes owners paying assessments for a share of real estate taxes, insurance premiums, maintenance or improvement of common elements. That describes what dues can fund. It does not require anyone to insure anything.

  • ✓The duty to insure. The council of co-owners shall insure the property against risks. Section 27-31-240.
  • ✓The duty to repair. Damaged property that Section 27-31-240 requires be insured must be repaired or replaced promptly by the council of co-owners, subject to the two exceptions above. Section 27-31-250(A).
  • ✓Who absorbs the shortfall. The cost of repair or replacement in excess of insurance proceeds and reserve must be considered a common expense. Section 27-31-250(B).
  • ✓How common expenses get shared. Co-owners contribute pro rata toward the expenses of administration and of maintenance and repair of the general common elements and, in the proper case, of the limited common elements. Section 27-31-190.
  • ✓What the Act still never mentions. The word deductible, and any liability of one owner to another. These are verified absences across the whole chapter, read on July 28, 2026.

SCENARIO BY SCENARIO

Five damage scenarios and what actually decides each one

Every scenario below reduces to the same three questions. Where does your unit legally end and the common element begin? Does the master policy cover the interior and improvements of the unit, or does it stop at the structure? And was the damaged part property the association had to insure, which is what pulls the repair duty and the common expense rule in Section 27-31-250 into play? The declaration answers the first. The policy answers the second. The statute and the declaration together answer the third.

We do not tell buyers what is typical here, because there is no reliable published answer for South Carolina and declarations genuinely differ from building to building. Anyone who tells you the HOA normally pays for a given loss is guessing. What follows is what we read for in each case.

  • ✓Water from the unit above. Two different questions get confused here. One is which policy repairs your ceiling, drywall and finishes. The other is whether the owner above owes you anything. The Horizontal Property Act does not assign one owner liability to another at all, so that second question turns on the declaration and on general law, and it is a question for an attorney.
  • ✓A roof leak. Roofs are named in the statute as a general common element, so keeping the roof up is a common expense shared by all owners. If the roof is property the association had to insure, Section 27-31-250 also puts the repair duty on the council and makes any cost above insurance proceeds and reserve a common expense. Whether the same is true of the damaged finishes in the unit below depends on how far the declaration and the master policy carry the insured property into the unit.
  • ✓A burst pipe inside a common wall. Main walls are listed as general common elements. The pipe inside one may or may not be, depending on how the declaration draws the boundary of the apartment and whether that line serves one unit or several. The declaration's description of the apartment is the controlling text.
  • ✓Hurricane damage to the building envelope. This is where the most money is at stake, and it gets its own section below.
  • ✓Damage to your own finishes, cabinets and flooring. This turns on whether the master policy covers the interior and improvements of the unit. Both loan investors now treat that as a straight yes or no question, and so should you.

THE DEDUCTIBLE

The master policy deductible decides how much of a covered loss lands on owners

A master policy pays a covered loss minus its deductible. Someone absorbs that deductible. The Horizontal Property Act never uses the word, but for a condominium it answers the question in substance. If the damaged part is property that Section 27-31-240 required the association to insure, Section 27-31-250(B) makes the cost of repair or replacement in excess of insurance proceeds and reserve a common expense, and Section 27-31-190 spreads common expenses pro rata. A deductible is exactly that kind of cost. Note the order in the statute. Insurance proceeds and reserve go first, and only the excess becomes a common expense.

Two things keep that from being the end of it. The statute does not define how far the insured property reaches into a unit, and it does not address repair obligations for parts of the building the association was not required to insure. So you still have to read the declaration. But do not accept a flat claim that South Carolina law is silent on the shortfall, because it is not.

The mortgage side confirms this cost does reach individual owners. Fannie Mae requires a borrower's individual policy, in projects where per unit deductibles are large enough, to include coverage for master property insurance policy deductible assessments levied on the unit owner by the HOA or co-op corporation for the applicable perils, plus loss assessment coverage in an amount sufficient to cover assessments in excess of 5 percent of the master property insurance policy coverage amount, divided by the number of units.

Both investors cap the deductible they will accept. Fannie Mae's maximum allowable deductible for all required property insurance perils is 5 percent of the master property insurance coverage amount, and where a policy carries multiple deductibles, such as a separate windstorm deductible or one for a specific element like the roof, the total applicable to a single occurrence must be no greater than 5 percent. Freddie Mac's per occurrence limit is also 5 percent, measured against the limit maintained for building coverage.

Freddie Mac changed its per unit rule in Bulletin 2026-C, dated March 18, 2026. For mortgages with application received dates on or after July 1, 2026, a per unit deductible in a master policy may not exceed $50,000 per unit. Where the master policy has one, the owner's HO-6 limit must be at least the greater of the amount sufficient to repair the unit to at least its condition prior to the loss, or the amount of that per unit deductible. Freddie also capped the HO-6 deductible itself at the greater of 5 percent of the coverage limit or $2,500.

Read those as financing rules, not as consumer protection. They govern whether a lender will lend on a unit in that building. They do not limit what your association may assess you. They still matter to you for a practical reason. If a building's deductible structure sits outside them, financing gets harder for you now and for your buyer later. One question worth putting to a board or manager in writing: both Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac allow an association to buy a deductible buy-back policy to meet their deductible requirements, so if a building carries a large deductible, ask whether the association has considered one.

HURRICANE AND NAMED STORM

Hurricane damage is where the deductible structure matters most

Wind is the loss that turns a master policy deductible into a real bill for owners on this coast. Fannie Mae's rules expressly contemplate a master policy carrying a separate deductible that applies to windstorms, which tells you the structure is ordinary rather than unusual.

Named storm is an objective trigger rather than an insurer's judgement call. Freddie Mac defines it by reference to storms designated by the U.S. National Weather Service or the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration by a name or number, and makes named storms a required component of windstorm coverage.

The market context is documented by the state, not by us. In its 2025 Status Report on the South Carolina Coastal Property Insurance Market, dated January 31, 2026, the Department of Insurance wrote that condominium associations with wooden buildings over fifty years old and located close to the ocean have been experiencing significant challenges finding affordable coverage.

The same report tracks associations leaving the state wind pool. The South Carolina Wind and Hail Underwriting Association began 2024 providing wind coverage to 70 condo associations, representing 735 individual buildings. At January 31, 2025 that fell to 20 associations with 175 buildings. By August 1, 2025 it was seven associations representing 23 buildings, and the numbers remained unchanged as of year end 2025. The department states these associations found better wind coverage at a lower price in the admitted marketplace.

None of that answers who pays. What it tells a buyer is that the wind portion of a building's insurance has been repriced recently, so the declarations page you are handed may be new and may not match what an owner tells you from memory. Ask when the master policy last renewed and what happened to the wind deductible at that renewal.

WHEN YOU DISAGREE

There is no state referee if you and the association disagree about a repair

Repairs are already one of the most common things South Carolina owners complain about. In the Department of Consumer Affairs 2026 HOA report, covering calendar year 2025, concerns regarding maintenance and repairs were 11.7 percent of 1,104 concerns raised across 452 reported complaints. The largest category, at 16.8 percent, was failure to adhere to or enforce covenants and bylaws, which cuts both ways. It counts associations that did not enforce as well as associations that enforced too hard.

Horry County produced 31 percent of all HOA complaints reported in the state and led per capita at 0.38 per 1,000 residents. Georgetown County was second per capita at 0.24.

Filing a complaint does less than owners expect. The department states that its complaint process is one of voluntary mediation, and that it cannot force an HOA or homeowner to participate in the complaint process, require a specific outcome or attempt to arbitrate the dispute.

The court route has hard limits. Section 27-30-160 gives the magistrates court concurrent jurisdiction to adjudicate monetary disputes arising under that article of the Homeowners Association Act, provided the dispute meets the jurisdictional requirements of Section 22-3-10, and the magistrates court civil limit is $7,500. A magistrate also has no cognizance of a civil action when the title to real property comes in question. So a disputed repair bill can fit there. A lien fight arising from it does not.

This page is general information, not legal advice. If an association has refused a repair you believe it owes, that is a conversation for a South Carolina attorney.

BEFORE YOU OFFER

What to read before you offer on a condo

Every question on this page is answerable before you are committed, from documents that already exist. The problem is timing. In the state's 2026 report only 32 percent of complainants indicated they received a copy of the governing documents prior to purchase, while 53 percent indicated receiving the documents after purchase.

The recording office is a real route to those documents. Section 27-30-130(A) makes recording in the clerk of court's, Register of Mesne Conveyance or register of deeds office in the county where the property is located a condition of a homeowners association's governing documents being enforceable. So the declaration for a South Carolina community is a recorded public document.

Do not count on asking instead. The Homeowners Association Act gives a buyer no document right at all. Section 27-30-130(B) reaches only rules, regulations and amendments to rules and regulations, it runs to a member who requests them, and it does not apply where those rules are posted in a conspicuous place in a common area in the community or are available on an Internet website maintained by the association. Section 27-30-150 reaches only the annual budget and membership lists, again for members, and only for associations not subject to the South Carolina Nonprofit Corporation Act. A buyer under contract is not a member, so the recording office is often faster than asking.

One caution on vocabulary. The industry uses shorthand names for how a master policy is written, and you will see them in listings and agent emails. No primary source we opened, at Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac or the South Carolina Department of Insurance, treats those names as defined categories. All of them frame it as a two-way question instead. Does the master policy cover the interior and improvements of the unit, or not. Ask the two-way question and get the answer from the policy, not from a label.

  • ✓The declaration's description of the apartment. The master deed must give a general description of each apartment expressing its area, location and any other data necessary for its identification, and the recorded floor plans must show the dimensions, area and location of each apartment. This is the text that decides where your unit ends.
  • ✓Whether the master policy covers the interior and improvements of the unit. Answer it from the policy itself. Fannie Mae Lender Letter LL-2026-03 and Freddie Mac Bulletin 2026-C now apply the same two-part test for application dates on or after July 1, 2026. A unit owners policy is required when the master policy does not cover all or any portion of the interior of the unit or the improvements to the unit, or when the master policy includes a per unit deductible.
  • ✓The master policy declarations page. Look for the per occurrence deductible and for any separate windstorm or named storm deductible. Then ask the association in writing how it has handled a deductible in past losses, because Section 27-31-250(B) points that cost at all owners as a common expense while the declaration draws the boundary the rule applies to.
  • ✓Your own HO-6 loss assessment coverage. Fannie Mae treats coverage for master policy deductible assessments as a separate item from loss assessment coverage generally, so do not assume one includes the other. Have your agent confirm both in writing.
  • ✓Any open claim or pending assessment. A loss that has already happened but has not yet been paid for is the one that arrives after you close.

Common questions

Who Pays for Damage FAQ

Who pays for water damage in a condo in South Carolina?

Two statutes answer part of it. Section 27-31-240 requires the council of co-owners to insure the property. Section 27-31-250 requires the council to repair insured property that is damaged, and says the cost of repair or replacement in excess of insurance proceeds and reserve must be considered a common expense, which Section 27-31-190 then shares pro rata among owners. The recorded declaration still decides where your unit ends and how far the insured property reaches into it. Read the declaration, the master policy and your HO-6 policy in that order.

Does the HOA pay for water damage from the unit above me?

Two separate questions are in play. One is which policy repairs your ceiling and finishes, and whether the damaged part was property the association had to insure, because Section 27-31-250 puts the repair duty on the council and makes the cost above insurance proceeds and reserve a common expense. The other is whether the owner above owes you money. The Horizontal Property Act contains no provision assigning one owner liability for damage to another owner's apartment, verified by reading the full chapter on July 28, 2026. That second question is one for a South Carolina attorney. This page is general information, not legal advice.

Who pays the condo master policy deductible?

The word deductible does not appear anywhere in the Horizontal Property Act, verified across the full chapter on July 28, 2026. The statute still reaches the answer another way. Section 27-31-250(B) provides that the cost of repair or replacement in excess of insurance proceeds and reserve must be considered a common expense, and Section 27-31-190 makes co-owners contribute toward common expenses pro rata. A deductible on a covered loss to insured property is that kind of cost. How far the insured property reaches into your unit comes from the declaration, so read it before you rely on any general answer.

Does the condo association have to insure the building in South Carolina?

Yes, for a condominium. Section 27-31-240 of the South Carolina Horizontal Property Act provides that the council of co-owners shall insure the property against risks, without prejudice to the right of each co-owner to insure his apartment on his own account and for his own benefit. That is the entire duty to buy insurance. It sets no coverage amount, no deductible limit and no list of perils. Section 27-31-250 then adds a duty to repair insured property that is damaged. A non-condominium HOA is different again: the Homeowners Association Act contains no insurance requirement at all.

Do I need HO-6 insurance if the condo association has a master policy?

Often yes, and your lender may require it. Fannie Mae Lender Letter LL-2026-03 and Freddie Mac Bulletin 2026-C, both dated March 18, 2026, adopted the same two-part test for application dates on or after July 1, 2026. A unit owners policy is required when the master policy does not cover all or any portion of the interior of the unit or the improvements to the unit, or when the master policy includes a per unit deductible. Freddie Mac also sets the HO-6 limit at the greater of the amount sufficient to repair the unit to at least its condition prior to the loss, or the amount of the per unit deductible.

Who pays for hurricane damage to a condo building?

The building's master policy responds, and its deductible decides how much lands on owners. For a South Carolina condominium, Section 27-31-250(B) treats the cost of repair above insurance proceeds and reserve as a common expense, shared pro rata under Section 27-31-190. Fannie Mae expressly contemplates a master policy carrying a separate deductible that applies to windstorms, and caps the total deductibles for a single occurrence at 5 percent of the coverage amount. Freddie Mac limits a per unit deductible to $50,000 per unit for applications received on or after July 1, 2026. Named storm means a storm designated by the National Weather Service or NOAA by a name or number.
