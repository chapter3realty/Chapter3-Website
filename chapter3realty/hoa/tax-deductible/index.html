HOA fees and tax

Are HOA fees tax deductible?
The answer turns on how you use the property

By Timmy Fredrick Nash, Broker-in-Charge, 30+ years on the Grand Strand · Reviewed by Devin Day, Operations Officer · Updated July 28, 2026

No, not on a home you live in. IRS Publication 530 lists "Homeowners association fees, condominium association fees, or common charges" under the heading Nondeductible payments. Renting the property out changes the treatment, and the answer then turns on the property type. For a rented condominium, Publication 527 says you can deduct dues or assessments paid for maintenance of the common elements. For a detached house in an HOA subdivision the IRS has no passage naming the fee, so any deduction rests on the general rule that ordinary and necessary expenses of producing rental income are deductible. Personal use splits the expense by day count. This is general information, not tax advice. Ask a CPA.

PRIMARY RESIDENCE

On a home you live in, HOA fees are not deductible

The IRS answers this one directly. Publication 530 is the IRS guide for homeowners. It carries a heading called Nondeductible payments, introduced by the sentence: You can't deduct any of the following items. One item on that list, word for word, is: Homeowners association fees, condominium association fees, or common charges. That wording reaches a regular HOA due and a condominium regime fee, which is the monthly charge a condominium owner pays the regime for upkeep of the shared property, on a home you occupy. A master association charge falls under the same common charges language.

Publication 530 rules the charge out a second time, in a different place, for a different question. Some owners ask whether the assessment can be claimed as a real estate tax, since it pays for things a town might otherwise pay for. Under the heading Items You Can't Deduct as Real Estate Taxes, the publication answers: You can't deduct these assessments because the homeowners' association, rather than a state or local government, imposes them. The disqualifier is who imposes the charge, not what the money buys.

One widely repeated claim is worth flagging, because we could not verify it. A lot of articles say an HOA special assessment for a capital improvement adds to the basis of your home, which is the figure your gain is measured against when you sell. Publication 530 does not say that. Its Adjusted Basis table lists assessments for local improvements, the kind a local government levies for streets, sidewalks, or water and sewer systems, among the items that increase basis, and the IRS notes that the table is not intended to be all inclusive. We found no IRS text extending it to an association assessment. If it matters to your numbers, put the question to a CPA rather than to a website.

  • ✓Repairs to home. The same nondeductible list that names association fees also names repairs to home.
  • ✓Insurance on your home. That list item reads: Insurance, including fire and comprehensive coverage, and title insurance.
  • ✓Who imposes it. An association is not a state or local government, which is the stated reason its assessment is not a real estate tax.

RENTAL PROPERTY

On a rental the answer changes, and for a condominium the IRS says so directly

Publication 527 is the IRS guide for residential rental property. Under the heading 4. Special Situations it has a subheading called Condominiums. The text reads: Special rules apply if you rent your condominium to others. You can deduct as rental expenses all the expenses discussed in chapters 1 and 2. In addition, you can deduct any dues or assessments paid for maintenance of the common elements. That is the IRS, in its own words, on a rented condominium.

Notice the framing, because it matters. Publication 527 never uses the term HOA. We searched the full 2025 text on 28 July 2026. The words homeowners association, HOA and common charges do not appear in it, and neither does the word association. The publication reaches this subject only through condominiums and cooperatives, and it describes the entity as a special corporation that is organized to take care of the common elements. That framing reaches condominiums directly. It does not reach a detached house in an HOA subdivision.

A detached house inside an HOA subdivision is a different case. There the IRS has no passage naming the fee at all. The deduction rests instead on the general rule that ordinary and necessary expenses of producing rental income are deductible. It is an application of a general rule, not an IRS statement about HOA dues, and it is worth saying it that way to your preparer. Your CPA will tell you whether that position fits your facts.

There is also no printed line for the fee on the form. On the 2025 Schedule E, lines 5 through 18 are the fourteen named expense lines, running from advertising through depreciation, and none of them is HOA or condominium dues. Line 19 is labeled Other (list). The 2025 instructions define it as: Enter on line 19 any ordinary and necessary expenses not listed on lines 5 through 18. Those instructions never mention homeowners associations, HOA or common charges either.

One more precision point, because older articles get it wrong. Many of them cite IRS Publication 535, Business Expenses, for the ordinary and necessary standard. The IRS has discontinued that publication. Its guide to business expense resources carries the note: We have discontinued Publication 535, Business Expenses; the last revision was for 2022. If a page you are reading cites Publication 535 as current guidance, that page has not been refreshed in years.

PART YEAR RENTAL

If you use the property yourself, the fee is split by days

This case applies if you use the unit yourself for part of the year and rent it for the rest. Publication 527 sets the rule in Chapter 5: If you have any personal use of a dwelling unit (including a vacation home) that you rent, you must divide your expenses between rental use and personal use. In general, your rental expenses will be no more than your total expenses multiplied by a fraction, the denominator of which is the total number of days the dwelling unit is used and the numerator of which is the total number of days actually rented at a fair rental price. A fair rental price is, in the publication's words, generally the amount of rent that a person who isn't related to you would be willing to pay.

The IRS says your expenses there. It does not name HOA or condominium dues. Applying the fraction to the fee is a correct use of a general rule, not something the IRS has written about the fee itself. Two counting rules decide the fraction. A day the unit is rented at a fair rental price is a day of rental use even if you also used it that day. A day the unit is available but not actually rented is not a day of rental use.

A second test runs alongside the split, and it decides whether your deductions get capped at all. Publication 527 says you use a dwelling unit as a home during the tax year if you use it for personal purposes more than the greater of 14 days, or 10% of the total days it is rented to others at a fair rental price. The first counting rule does not apply to this test. Publication 527 carves it out: if a dwelling unit is used for personal purposes on a day it is rented at a fair rental price, do not count that day as a day of rental use in applying the 10% test, and instead count it as a day of personal use. So a day that is both rented and used by you helps you in the expense split and counts against you in the home test.

If the unit counts as a home under that test, Publication 527 caps the deduction. Renting a dwelling unit that is considered a home is not a passive activity, and rental expenses above your rental income cannot be used to offset income from other sources. The excess carries forward to the next year and is treated as a rental expense for the same property.

So the calendar decides the number. Keep a written log of every day the unit was rented at a fair rental price and every day you or your family used it, kept through the year rather than reconstructed in April. Your CPA needs the counts, not your recollection.

  • ✓A rented day counts even if you were there. Any day the unit is rented at a fair rental price is a day of rental use, even if you used the unit for personal purposes that day. The IRS adds a parenthetical: this rule doesn't apply when determining whether you used the unit as a home.
  • ✓An empty day does not count. Any day the unit is available for rent but not actually rented is not a day of rental use. Having it listed is not the same as having it rented.
  • ✓Work days are not personal days. Any day you spend working substantially full time repairing and maintaining the property is not counted as a day of personal use, even if family members use it for recreation that same day. The IRS adds not improving in parentheses, so improvement work does not get this treatment.
  • ✓Used as a home but rented less than 15 days. The IRS says the activity should not be reported on Schedule E and you are not required to report the rental income and rental expenses from it. Because Publication 530 puts the fee on the nondeductible list, that year produces no deduction for it at all.
  • ✓Used as a home and rented 15 days or more. A net profit means you deduct all of your rental expenses and do not need Worksheet 5-1. A net loss means the deduction for certain rental expenses is limited, and the IRS sends you to Worksheet 5-1 to figure the deductible amount and any carryover to next year.

SPECIAL ASSESSMENTS

A special assessment is treated by what it paid for, not by its size

Publication 527 answers this for a rented condominium in two sentences: You can't deduct special assessments you pay to a condominium management corporation for improvements. However, you may be able to recover your share of the cost of any improvement by taking depreciation. Depreciation means deducting a cost a piece at a time over a set number of years rather than all at once. So an improvement assessment is not a current deduction. It is capitalized, which means added to the cost of the property, and recovered over time.

The test for what counts as an improvement sits in Chapter 1 of the same publication: You must capitalize any expense you pay to improve your rental property. An expense is for an improvement if it results in a betterment to your property, restores your property, or adapts your property to a new or different use. Table 1-1 of that publication gives examples of improvements. A new roof is one of the examples in that table.

Here is the limit of what this page will tell you. The IRS contrasts dues or assessments paid for maintenance, which are deductible on a rented condominium, against special assessments for improvements, which are not. It does not use the word repair anywhere in that passage. So we will not tell you that an assessment levied to repair something is currently deductible. Sorting a specific assessment against that test is a judgment call on the facts, and it belongs to a CPA who can see how the association itself described the work.

Getting that description is real work, not a formality. The assessment resolution, the board minutes and the reserve study, which is the report estimating what major components will cost to replace and when, are the evidence your CPA will reason from, because they say what the money was for. Our page on HOA special assessments covers what triggers one and how to spot it before you make an offer.

HOME OFFICE

The IRS does not address HOA fees in its home office rules

Publication 587 is the IRS guide to business use of your home. We read the full 2025 text on 28 July 2026 and searched it. The words homeowner, homeowners association, HOA and common charges do not appear anywhere in it, and neither does the word association. The single occurrence of condominium is in the introduction, where the IRS defines what counts as a home. That is a verified absence, and it is the most honest thing this page can say about the question.

What Publication 587 does give you is a classification. Expenses are direct, indirect, or unrelated. Indirect expenses are, in the publication's own table, expenses for keeping up and running your entire home, deductible based on the percentage of your home used for business, with the examples given as insurance, utilities, and general repairs. Association fees are not named as an example in any of the three categories.

Treating the fee as an indirect expense is a reasonable reading of that table. It is not something the IRS has stated. If you run a business from a home in an HOA and the amount is material, ask a CPA, and ask them to write down the position they are taking and why.

We get the documents; a CPA takes the tax position

This page is general information about how the IRS treats these fees. It is not tax advice, and none of it is written about your situation. Chapter3 Realty is a real estate brokerage. We are not accountants and we do not prepare returns. Take your numbers to a CPA.

What we can do is put the documents in front of you that the CPA will ask for. Before you go under contract we request the association budget, the recent financial statements, the assessment history and the meeting minutes. Those tell you what the fee covers, whether an assessment has already been voted, and what the association says an assessment is for. On a resale condominium in South Carolina, the Horizontal Property Act, which is the state statute governing condominiums, also requires the council of co-owners, meaning the body of unit owners acting together, to issue to any purchaser on request a statement of the amounts the seller owes. The statute limits the purchaser's liability to the amount set out in that statement, so we ask for it.

Two things are worth doing early rather than late. Start the day log on day one if you intend to rent, because the split for a part year rental is decided by counts you cannot reconstruct later. And read the rental rules in the governing documents before you model any rental income at all, because what the documents permit is the limit on what you can rent.

If you are still working out what the fee itself pays for, the HOA hub is the place to start. If you are weighing whether to rent the unit, our long term rental and short term rental income pages go through the trade offs.

Common questions

Tax treatment FAQ

Are HOA fees tax deductible on my primary residence?

No. IRS Publication 530, the guide for homeowners, has a heading called Nondeductible payments, and one item on that list reads: Homeowners association fees, condominium association fees, or common charges. The same publication separately explains that you cannot claim the assessment as a real estate tax, because the association, rather than a state or local government, imposes it. That wording reaches a regular HOA due and a condominium regime fee on a home you occupy, and a master association charge falls under the same common charges language. This is general information and not tax advice. Confirm your own return with a CPA.

Are HOA fees deductible on a rental property?

For a rented condominium the IRS says so directly. Publication 527 states that you can deduct any dues or assessments paid for maintenance of the common elements. For a detached house in an HOA subdivision the IRS has no passage naming the fee at all, so the deduction rests on the general rule that ordinary and necessary expenses of producing rental income are deductible. That is an application of a general rule rather than an IRS statement about HOA dues. Have your CPA take the position and record it.

Can I deduct HOA fees on a condo I rent out part of the year?

Only the rental share. Publication 527 says that if you have any personal use of a dwelling unit you rent, you must divide your expenses between rental use and personal use, using a fraction whose numerator is the days actually rented at a fair rental price and whose denominator is the total days the unit is used. A day rented at a fair rental price is a rental day even if you also used it that day. A day the unit is available but not actually rented is not a rental day. Publication 527 carves out an exception: that first rule does not apply when you work out whether you used the unit as a home, and for that test a day both rented and used by you counts as a day of personal use.

Is an HOA special assessment tax deductible?

Not if it paid for an improvement. Publication 527 says you cannot deduct special assessments paid to a condominium management corporation for improvements, and that you may be able to recover your share by taking depreciation. The IRS test for an improvement is whether the work results in a betterment, restores the property, or adapts it to a new or different use, and its own examples of improvements include a new roof. The IRS does not use the word repair in that passage, so do not assume a repair assessment is currently deductible. Ask a CPA, with the association's own description of the work in hand.

Where do HOA fees go on Schedule E?

There is no printed line for them. On the 2025 Schedule E, lines 5 through 18 are the fourteen named expense lines, from advertising through depreciation, and none of them is HOA or condominium dues. Line 19 is labeled Other (list), and the 2025 instructions define it as any ordinary and necessary expenses not listed on lines 5 through 18. The Schedule E instructions never mention homeowners associations, HOA or common charges. Line 19 is where unlisted rental expenses go, and your preparer decides how to describe the entry.

Can I deduct HOA fees if I work from home?

The IRS does not answer this. We read the full 2025 text of Publication 587, Business Use of Your Home, on 28 July 2026. The words homeowner, homeowners association, HOA and common charges do not appear in it, and neither does the word association. What the publication does give is a split between direct, indirect and unrelated expenses, with indirect defined as expenses for keeping up and running your entire home and examples given as insurance, utilities and general repairs. Treating the fee as indirect is a reasonable reading, but the IRS has not said it. Ask a CPA.
