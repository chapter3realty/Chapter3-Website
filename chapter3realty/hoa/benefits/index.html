Are the dues worth it

Are HOA fees worth it
on the Grand Strand?

By Timmy Fredrick Nash, Broker-in-Charge, 30+ years on the Grand Strand · Reviewed by Devin Day, Operations Officer · Updated July 28, 2026

Whether HOA fees are worth it depends on the specific association, not on HOAs in general. In a South Carolina condominium the dues must buy insurance on the building, because the Horizontal Property Act, the state condominium statute, requires the owners' association to insure the property against risks. Freddie Mac requires that coverage at 100 percent of replacement cost, with roofs insured but no longer settled on that basis. No single owner buys that alone. The cost is control and money. South Carolina caps no dues, fines or increases, and the HOA Act's only notice rule, 48 hours before the meeting that decides a budget increase, does not apply to associations incorporated as nonprofits.

WHAT THE DUES BUY

In a South Carolina condominium, state law requires the association to insure the building

Start with the item you cannot buy on your own. The Horizontal Property Act, which is the South Carolina statute that governs condominiums, requires the council of co-owners, which is the owners' association in a condominium, to insure the property against risks. The same section preserves each co-owner's right to insure their own apartment on their own account and for their own benefit. The association's duty is statutory, not a service level a board picked, and your dues are how it gets paid.

Freddie Mac and Fannie Mae set the size of that policy. Freddie Mac's rule, recited in its May 2023 industry letter on condominium insurance, requires the association to insure the buildings and structures in the project and the common elements for 100 percent of their replacement cost. Common elements are the parts of the property every owner owns a share of, such as land, lobbies, elevators and service areas. One part of that rule changed on March 18, 2026. In Bulletin 2026-C Freddie Mac retired the requirement that roofs be covered on a replacement cost basis. The master policy must now provide coverage on a replacement cost basis excluding roofs, and roofs must be insured but do not have to be covered on that basis. Freddie Mac says it made the change in consultation with the Federal Housing Finance Agency and in alignment with Fannie Mae. Ask which basis the roof is settled on.

This matters to owners without a loan as well. If the association stops carrying coverage that meets these rules, the building can fall outside what Freddie Mac and Fannie Mae will buy, which narrows the pool of buyers who can finance a purchase there.

On this coast the cost of that coverage moves. In its 2025 status report on the coastal property insurance market, submitted January 31, 2026, the South Carolina Department of Insurance said condominium associations with wooden buildings over fifty years old and located close to the ocean have been experiencing significant challenges finding affordable coverage. The rest of that report is less bleak. The department put the state wind pool's share at 8 percent of dwellings in its territory, so 92 percent of dwelling owners were covered in the admitted market, which is the regular licensed market. It said the coastal property market appears more favorable than before. The number of condominium associations buying wind coverage from the state wind pool, the South Carolina Wind and Hail Underwriting Association, fell from 70 at the start of 2024 to seven at the end of 2025, and the report says those associations found better wind coverage at a lower price in the admitted market. Horry County still carries the largest wind pool exposure of any county, $2.35 billion of in force insured limits at December 31, 2025. Owning a detached house on the coast does not remove that risk. It puts all of it on one policy in your name.

The rest of the budget is easier to read. Exterior maintenance, the roof, elevators, pools and landscaping are all line items you can see. Some condominium budgets include utilities such as water, sewer and trash. Others do not. Do not assume either way. Read the budget. The insurance duty above sits in the condominium statute, so if you are buying in a subdivision rather than a condominium, check which kind of association you are joining first.

WHERE THE COVERAGE STOPS

The master policy can stop at the walls, and its deductible can be passed to owners

The association's policy does not automatically cover the inside of your unit. Both companies that buy most conventional mortgages write their rules around that gap, and both changed those rules on March 18, 2026 with the same effective date. For loan applications dated on or after July 1, 2026, Freddie Mac requires the lender to document that the borrower has an HO-6 unit owners policy, which is the condominium owner's own policy, when the master property insurance policy does not cover all or any portion of the interior of the unit or the improvements to the unit, or when the master policy includes a per unit deductible. Fannie Mae adopted the same two part test in Lender Letter LL-2026-03, issued the same day, on the same application date.

The size of that owner policy is set the same way. Under Freddie Mac's rule the HO-6 coverage limit must be at least the greater of the amount sufficient to repair the unit to at least its condition prior to the loss, or the amount of the per unit deductible if the master policy includes one. If the master policy includes a per unit deductible, the HO-6 policy must also cover all the perils that deductible applies to.

Deductibles are the second half of the answer. Freddie Mac limits the master policy deductible for required perils to 5 percent of the limit maintained for building coverage per occurrence. Separately, if the master policy includes a per unit deductible, that deductible may not exceed $50,000 per unit for applications dated on or after July 1, 2026. A per occurrence deductible and a per unit deductible are different things, and the per unit one is the one that lands on a single owner. That is why the HO-6 limit has to be at least as large as it.

So the dues do not remove the insurance cost. They share part of it and move part of it. Before you offer, get in writing what the master policy covers, what the deductible is, and whether that deductible applies per occurrence or per unit. What coverage costs in this market is a separate question and we cover it elsewhere.

THE CONTROL YOU GIVE UP

South Carolina law sets almost no limit on what an association can require

An association's authority here comes from its recorded governing documents, not from a state code of conduct. The Homeowners Association Act says that to be enforceable, the governing documents must be recorded in the clerk of court's, Register of Mesne Conveyance, or register of deeds office in the county where the property is located. Those are the county land records offices. The Act says almost nothing about what those documents may contain. The Department of Consumer Affairs states it plainly in its own annual report: South Carolina does not have a state specific law detailing how a homeowners association must operate.

So there is no statutory cap on dues. There is no statutory cap on fines. There is no required hearing, no required notice period before a fine, and no appeal panel. The Act's one notice rule is 48 hours in advance of the meeting in which a decision to raise the annual budget is made. That rule has a limit that matters. It does not apply to a homeowners association incorporated under the South Carolina Nonprofit Corporation Act, and the Department of Consumer Affairs says that Act applies to associations that have filed nonprofit articles of incorporation with the Secretary of State. For an association that has filed them, the 48 hour rule does not apply at all, and its meeting and notice rules come from the Nonprofit Corporation Act and the recorded documents instead. You can check the filing on the Secretary of State's business filings site.

The Act's access rights are narrower than most summaries suggest. Rules, regulations and amendments to rules and regulations are effective upon passage or adoption and must be made accessible to a member on that member's request, unless they are posted in a conspicuous place in a common area in the community or available on a website the association maintains. That provision covers rules. It does not reach the declaration, which is the recorded document that creates the association and its restrictions, and it does not reach the bylaws or the budget. A separate section applies the Nonprofit Corporation Act's document access provisions to associations not subject to that Act, and only for inspecting and copying the annual budget and the membership list. Every one of these rights runs to a member. A buyer under contract is not a member, so there is no statutory right to demand documents from the association before closing.

If you search this topic you will find a confident claim that South Carolina caps HOA fines at $100 and requires 14 days notice and a hearing before an adjudicatory panel. That is not law. It is bill language that has been filed in the South Carolina Senate three times and never passed: S.30 in the 2009 to 2010 session, S.218 in 2011 to 2012, and S.819 in 2013 to 2014. Each was referred to a subcommittee and died there. The chapter those bills proposed, Title 27 Chapter 52, does not exist in the South Carolina Code. Do not plan around it.

The remedies are narrow too. The Homeowners Association Act gives the magistrates court concurrent jurisdiction to adjudicate monetary disputes arising under that article, provided the dispute meets the magistrate jurisdiction limit, which the code sets at a sum claimed not exceeding $7,500. A magistrate has no cognizance of a civil action in which the title to real property comes in question, apart from a limited exception in the same chapter. A complaint to the Department of Consumer Affairs is voluntary mediation. The agency says it cannot force an HOA or homeowner to participate in the complaint process, require a specific outcome or attempt to arbitrate the dispute. In 2025, 68 percent of the complaints included in its report closed on receipt of what it calls an adequate business response. The agency defines that as a response that may not satisfy the wishes of the consumer but did respond to all points of contention and referred to a policy that supports the business's claim. Consumers were satisfied with the outcome in four complaints.

You are agreeing to private rules you did not write and cannot easily change, and the state will not resolve most disputes about them for you. Whether that is worth it depends on what those rules say, which is why they should be read before you offer. This is general information about South Carolina law, not legal advice. For a specific dispute, talk to a South Carolina attorney.

WHAT THE COMPLAINT DATA SHOWS

Horry County files more HOA complaints than any other county in South Carolina

The Department of Consumer Affairs publishes an annual HOA report. The 2026 edition covers calendar year 2025. The agency received 586 HOA complaints that year and excluded 134 of them. The largest group excluded was 78 complaints abandoned when the complainant did not submit the required supplemental questionnaire, followed by 39 duplicates, 4 referrals to the South Carolina Human Affairs Commission and 13 that did not meet the statutory requirements for inclusion. The report covers the remaining 452 complaints, filed against 339 associations and management companies. HOA complaints were 8.5 percent of every complaint the agency received in 2025, and are up 176 percent since 2019.

Horry County was first on both measures the report uses. It accounted for 31 percent of all HOA complaints reported, ahead of Richland at 12 percent and Greenville at 9 percent. Per capita it also led, at 0.38 complaints per 1,000 residents, with Georgetown County second at 0.24.

The top three concerns raised were failure to adhere to or enforce covenants and bylaws at 16.8 percent, concerns regarding maintenance and repairs at 11.7 percent, and ignored requests to access information or view documents at 9.5 percent. The first category is worded as failure to adhere to and/or enforce covenants and bylaws, so it covers an association not following its own documents as well as one not enforcing them. The report does not break the category down further.

Only 32 percent of complainants indicated they received a copy of the governing documents before they purchased. Fifty three percent received them after. Those figures describe people who filed complaints, not all buyers. Among that group, most did not have the rulebook until after they owned the property. That does not make dues a bad deal. It makes buying without reading the declaration a bad idea.

THE MONEY, HONESTLY

Dues are not deductible on a home you live in, and low dues are not automatically cheaper

The IRS is direct about the first part. Publication 530 lists homeowners association fees, condominium association fees, or common charges under the heading Nondeductible payments. It answers the follow up question separately, under the items you cannot deduct as real estate taxes: you cannot deduct these assessments because the homeowners association, rather than a state or local government, imposes them. The disqualifier is who charges you, not what the money pays for.

Rentals are treated differently, and the difference is worth knowing before you buy. Publication 527 addresses condominiums directly. If you rent your condominium to others you can deduct as rental expenses all the expenses discussed in chapters 1 and 2 of that publication, and in addition you can deduct any dues or assessments paid for maintenance of the common elements. The same passage draws a hard line at improvements. You cannot deduct special assessments you pay to a condominium management corporation for improvements, though you may be able to recover your share of the cost of an improvement by taking depreciation, which means deducting the cost in pieces over a set number of years. If you also use the unit yourself, the publication divides expenses using the ratio of days rented at a fair rental price to total days used.

There is no line on Schedule E called HOA dues. Rental expenses go on lines 5 through 19, and none of those lines names association fees. Line 19 is Other, and the instructions say to enter there any ordinary and necessary expenses not listed on lines 5 through 18. This is general information, not tax advice. Take your own figures to a CPA. It is here because the tax treatment changes the arithmetic on whether dues are worth it, and most comparisons ignore it.

Now the low dues question. An association that keeps dues down by underfunding reserves has not removed the cost of the repairs that are coming. That cost is still there, and it is paid by whoever owns the unit when the work is done. Freddie Mac is moving its requirement the other way. For loan applications received on or after January 4, 2027, the reserve allocation for capital expenditures and deferred maintenance rises from a minimum of 10 percent to a minimum of 15 percent of the annual budgeted assessment income. The same bulletin also requires the project's budget to include the highest recommended reserve allocation amount in the reserve study, which is the report that estimates what big repairs are coming and what they will cost.

One more line to check before closing. South Carolina's 2012 private transfer fee statute does not make an association transfer fee or capital contribution unenforceable. It excludes from the ban a fee or charge paid to an association and used exclusively for purposes authorized in the document, provided no portion of the fee is required to be passed through to a third party designated or identifiable by description in the document. A capital contribution is a one time payment to the association at closing, separate from regular dues. The authority to charge it still has to be in the recorded documents. The statute does not cap the amount and does not say who pays it, so the purchase contract decides. Confirm the allocation in writing before you sign.

WHEN IT IS WORTH IT

When the trade works, and when it does not

Every condominium in South Carolina has an association, because the Horizontal Property Act creates one. So for most of the condominium inventory on the Grand Strand the question is not whether to accept an association. It is which one. The test has four parts and all four can be run before you offer.

It stops being worth it when the dues buy less than they cost. Thin reserves next to visible deferred maintenance point to a repair bill that has not been assessed yet. If a rule blocks the use you intend, that property is worth less to you than the price suggests. If an association will not produce documents when you ask, you have learned something useful before you own anything.

None of this is a verdict on any particular association, and we will not publish one. What we do is read the recorded declaration, the bylaws, the current budget, the reserve study, the insurance certificate and the recent minutes, then tell you what they say and what they do not say. Then you decide whether the fee is worth it for that building and for your plan.

  • ✓The dues buy something you cannot buy alone. Insurance on the building, a roof over the whole structure, an elevator, a seawall. If the expensive parts are shared, sharing the cost is rational.
  • ✓The reserves are funded and the reserve study is current. A funded reserve is the difference between a predictable bill and a surprise one.
  • ✓The rules do not conflict with your plan. If you intend to rent, read the rental caps and lifestyle rules first rather than last. A cap you cannot live with makes every other number irrelevant.
  • ✓Control of the association has passed from the developer to the owners. Ask what the recorded documents say about when that transfer happens, and whether it has happened yet.

Common questions

Pros and cons FAQ

Are HOA fees worth it?

It depends on the association, and you can test it before you offer. Ask what the dues buy that you could not buy alone, such as insurance on the building, the roof, and the elevators. Then ask what they cost you in control, because South Carolina puts no statutory cap on dues or fines and leaves the rules to the recorded documents. If reserves are funded, the budget is readable, and the rules fit your plan, the fee is buying real work. If reserves are thin, the cost of the repairs that are coming has not gone away. It has only been left for a later bill.

Do HOA fees cover insurance on my condo?

They cover insurance on the building, not necessarily the inside of your unit. South Carolina's Horizontal Property Act requires the council of co-owners to insure the property against risks, and Freddie Mac requires the association to insure the buildings, structures and common elements for 100 percent of their replacement cost. Since Bulletin 2026-C in March 2026, roofs must be insured but no longer have to be covered on a replacement cost basis. Where the master policy stops is set by the declaration and by the policy itself. For applications dated on or after July 1, 2026, Freddie Mac and Fannie Mae both require the owner to carry a unit owners policy when the master policy does not cover all or any portion of the unit interior or the improvements, or when it includes a per unit deductible. Get both documents before you offer.

Are HOA fees tax deductible?

Not on a home you live in. IRS Publication 530 lists homeowners association fees, condominium association fees, or common charges under Nondeductible payments, and separately says you cannot deduct these assessments as real estate taxes because the homeowners association, rather than a state or local government, imposes them. On a rental condominium, Publication 527 says you can deduct any dues or assessments paid for maintenance of the common elements, but not special assessments paid to a condominium management corporation for improvements, and it says you may be able to recover your share of an improvement by taking depreciation instead. This is general information, not tax advice. Take your own numbers to a CPA.

Can an HOA raise fees without telling owners in South Carolina?

The Homeowners Association Act requires notice to homeowners at least 48 hours in advance of the meeting in which a decision to raise the annual budget is made. That requirement has a limit that matters. It does not apply to an association incorporated under the South Carolina Nonprofit Corporation Act, which covers associations that have filed nonprofit articles of incorporation with the Secretary of State. For those, meeting and notice rules come from the Nonprofit Corporation Act and from the recorded documents instead. Either way, 48 hours is the figure in the HOA Act, not the 14 days that circulates online, and there is no statutory cap on the size of an increase. Check the Secretary of State's business filings, then read the declaration and bylaws.

Is there a limit on HOA fines in South Carolina?

No. There is no statutory cap on fines, no required hearing, and no required notice period before a fine. The authority to fine comes from the recorded governing documents, which the Homeowners Association Act makes enforceable only if they are recorded in the county where the property sits. The widely repeated claim that South Carolina caps fines at $100 and requires 14 days notice before an adjudicatory panel is bill language that has never been enacted. The same text was filed in the Senate three times, as S.30 in 2009 to 2010, S.218 in 2011 to 2012 and S.819 in 2013 to 2014, and all three died in committee. The chapter they proposed does not exist in the South Carolina Code.

What happens if I do not pay my HOA fees in South Carolina?

In a condominium, unpaid sums assessed for your share of common expenses become a lien on the apartment under the Horizontal Property Act. That lien comes before all other liens except tax liens on the apartment in favor of an assessing unit, and mortgage and other liens that are duly recorded, and it can be foreclosed in like manner as a mortgage of real property. Unpaid amounts also follow the unit on sale, which is why the council of co-owners must issue to a purchaser, on request, a statement of the amounts due by the seller. Outside a condominium, the Homeowners Association Act does not create a lien, so any lien right comes only from the recorded declaration.
