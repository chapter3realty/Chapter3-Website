HOA Guide

The buyer of a Myrtle Beach condo can owe the seller's special assessment
What one costs, who pays it, and how we find it in the board's minutes.

By Devin Day, Operations Officer · Reviewed by Tim Nash, Broker-in-Charge · Updated November 12, 2026

An agent reads a Myrtle Beach condo board's meeting minutes for you, so you know about a coming special assessment before you make an offer.

The short answer

A special assessment is a one-time bill from a condo association, which all the owners belong to, on top of the regular dues. It pays for a cost the yearly budget and the reserve fund, the money saved for big repairs, do not cover. South Carolina law puts no limit on the amount. Your share is your unit's percentage, set in the condo's founding papers, recorded when the condo was created.

If a seller leaves an assessment unpaid, the association can collect it from the buyer too. The buyer's limit is the amount on the association's written statement of what the seller owes. We get that statement before closing, the day the sale is final.

What is a special assessment on a condo?

A special assessment is a bill the association sends every owner, once, for a cost its budget and its reserve fund cannot pay. Dues pay the yearly budget. The reserve fund is money the association saves for big repairs, such as a roof. A special assessment pays the rest.

When a part of a condo building the association must insure is damaged, the repair is paid in this order.

  1. 1The building's insuranceThe association must insure the building.
  2. 2The reserve fundThe money the association has saved for big repairs.
  3. 3The ownersThe rest, divided among the owners. This can be a special assessment.

Who pays to repair damage to an insured part of a South Carolina condo building, in order. A building's bylaws can split the owners' part another way. The board can bill that part as a special assessment, or add it to the yearly budget.

In a neighborhood of houses, the recorded rules of the homeowners association (HOA) say whether it can charge one. Our page on South Carolina's HOA laws covers both kinds.

How much is a condo special assessment?

The size depends on the repair, what the reserve fund holds, and your unit's percentage. At one Pawleys Island condo, the board proposed about $65,000 to $88,000 a unit. In our example below, it is $5,850 to $10,350.

What did one Pawleys Island condo's board ask each owner to pay?

At Shipyard Village, a condo in Pawleys Island, south of Myrtle Beach, the board said two of its buildings needed extensive repairs. It said every unit's windows and sliding doors had to be replaced, at $12 million to $13 million. A consultant found corroded reinforcing steel and an outer shell that was not weather tight.

The special assessment its board proposed in 2009 for each unit in two of its buildings.

The owners voted it down. The board then put the repair cost into the next two yearly budgets instead.

Owners sued the association, and the South Carolina Supreme Court ruled on the case, Fisher v. Shipyard Village. For a buyer, this means a no vote can still lead to a bigger yearly budget, so the budget is on our list too.

How old are the condo buildings for sale on the Grand Strand?

Most are more than 25 years old. About two in three condos for sale here are in buildings built before 2000.

How long do a condo building's roof, elevator and windows last?

The U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) publishes how long building parts last when it plans repairs.

A building built before 1990 is older than HUD expects a flat or shingle roof, elevator machinery or aluminum windows to last. That does not mean those parts were never replaced. We find out which ones were from the minutes and the reserve study, if there is one. A reserve study is a list of the building's big parts, with when each needs replacing and what it will cost.

Can a condo HOA charge a special assessment without a vote in South Carolina?

Yes, if the building's bylaws allow it. Under South Carolina's condo law, each building's recorded bylaws say how owners are billed. Any limit on the amount is in those bylaws and the founding papers.

Does South Carolina require a reserve study or regular structural inspections?

No. The state's condo and HOA laws require neither.

  • ✕No reserve studyrequired
  • ✕No periodic structural inspectionrequired

In the Shipyard Village case, the South Carolina Supreme Court said a court will not overrule a board that acts within its powers and in good faith. A court can rule against a board that breaks its own founding papers or bylaws.

Do owners in Horry County dispute special assessments?

Yes. More of South Carolina's HOA complaints are about associations in Horry County than in any other county. Associations there are also the subject of about 2 in 5 of the complaints over fees or special assessments. For a buyer, that is a reason to see how a building's last assessment was voted. That vote is in the minutes we read.

How can I tell if a condo has a special assessment coming?

We read the building's papers, the same ones Fannie Mae, which buys home loans from lenders, tells a lender to read. The right to see them belongs to owners, so we ask the seller for them before you make an offer.

  1. Board minutes. An association that is a nonprofit corporation must keep minutes of every board meeting, so a repair being priced shows up there.
  2. The association's list of special assessments and needed repairs. It shows what is billed now and what repairs are planned.
  3. Any engineer's or inspection report. A lender must read a structural or mechanical inspection done in the last 3 years, and so do we.
  4. The reserve study, if there is one, against the building's age. We compare what the fund holds with the parts HUD expects to be worn out. If a building has none, the minutes and the budget carry more weight. Our reserves page shows how much a building should have saved.
  5. The budget and the record of every expense. A condo board must keep a detailed record of every receipt and expense, open to all owners.
  6. The seller's disclosure. It asks: "Has the owners association levied any special assessments or similar charges?" It also asks about "any known common area problems".

In most resales the seller fills in that disclosure form before a contract. The answer can be yes, no or No Representation, which means the seller does not know.

What if the papers come after I sign?

Then we read them inside the contract's due diligence period. The standard South Carolina sales contract has one, for a length you and the seller agree on. Inside it, you can end the contract for any reason, with written notice and the fee the contract names. After it ends, that right ends.

Our page on HOA documents explains each paper a buyer gets.

Who pays a special assessment when a condo sells?

The seller pays what was billed before closing. It comes out of the sale price.

If the seller does not pay, the association can collect from the buyer too, for everything owed up to closing. The buyer owes no more of the seller's bill than the association's written statement shows. If it shows nothing owed, the buyer owes none of it. We get that statement and find out what the seller owes before closing, so you do not get stuck with it. Our page on that statement and its fee says what it costs.

When owners vote for one before closing and the bill comes after, the association bills you, as the owner then, unless the offer says the seller pays it. The offer can say the same for installments due after closing.

If the minutes show one coming, we tell you. Before you offer, you can offer less, ask the seller for a credit at closing, or not make an offer. The seller's disclosure form asks only about assessments already levied, so the minutes are where a coming one shows up.

Can a special assessment stop a condo loan?

Yes. If a building's special assessment pays for a critical repair that has not been fixed, Fannie Mae will not buy a loan on any unit in that building. For a buyer, that rules out a regular loan your lender plans to sell to Fannie Mae, until the repair is done. Cash still works. FHA, VA and other loans have their own rules, and our page on condos most lenders will not lend on explains them.

Which repairs does Fannie Mae count as critical?

  • Repairs to items such as sea walls, elevators, waterproofing, stairwells, balconies, foundations, electrical systems and parking structures.
  • Mold, water intrusion and damaging leaks in the buildings.
  • A repair over $10,000 per unit, due within 12 months, with no money set aside for it.
  • A full or partial evacuation order for an unsafe condition. The building is ineligible until it is safe.

Damage to only one or a few units does not count.

Can I refuse to pay a special assessment, or pay it over time?

No owner can refuse a lawful share. Not using the shared areas, or giving up the unit, does not end it.

No South Carolina statute gives an owner a right to a payment plan. Whether one is offered is the board's choice.

What happens if a special assessment goes unpaid in South Carolina?

An unpaid condo assessment is a lien on the unit. If the unit is sold to pay its debts, property taxes and the mortgage are paid first, then the association. The association can foreclose its lien in court like a mortgage, or sue for the money without foreclosing. Our page on HOA liens and foreclosure has each step.

A neighbor's unpaid share can become every owner's cost. When a lender forecloses on a unit, some of that unit's unpaid assessments are split among all the owners.

Association assessments are not deductible as real estate tax, because an association, not a government, charges them. Our page on whether HOA fees are tax deductible has the detail.

Common questions

Special assessment questions

Can the board change my unit's percentage to raise my share?

No. Your unit's percentage is set in the condo's founding papers. It cannot be changed without every owner's agreement, except as a phased condo's founding papers provide or when owners vote not to rebuild a unit.

How much say does one owner have in a vote on a special assessment?

As much as the unit's percentage. Where owners vote, a majority means owners holding at least 51 percent of the building's value, not 51 percent of the units.

Can a condo board borrow money instead of charging a special assessment?

Yes, if the association is a nonprofit corporation and its articles do not forbid it. Under South Carolina law, it may borrow money and pledge its income to repay the loan.

Does a special assessment matter if I pay cash?

Yes. You owe your share like every other owner. Fannie Mae's loan rules do not stop a cash purchase, but they apply to a later buyer of your unit who needs a loan.
