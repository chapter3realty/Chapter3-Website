Is a POA the same as an HOA in South Carolina?
Fees by kind of home, and why some homes pay two associations.

By Devin Day, Operations Officer · Reviewed by Tim Nash, Broker-in-Charge · Updated November 14, 2026

Yes. In South Carolina law they are the same. Also what you own in a Grand Strand condo, who fixes the roof, and how a condo can change the loan.

The short answer

Yes. In South Carolina, a property owners association (POA) and a homeowners association (HOA) are the same thing in law. Under the South Carolina Homeowners Association Act, a group is an HOA when papers filed with the county make the owners pay it dues.

The kind of home changes the fee. On the Grand Strand, the typical fee on a condo for sale is about $650 a month, and on a house about $95. Condo owners share the building, and the association insures it.

What is the difference between an HOA and a POA?

You may read that a POA covers more kinds of property than an HOA, such as empty lots or shops. Under either name, the group's papers on file with the county list what it runs and what its owners pay.

If you fall behind on dues, a POA can put a lien on your home when its papers allow one. A lien is a claim on the home for the money you owe. An HOA can do the same. Of the more than 500 associations that recorded a lien for unpaid charges in Horry County in 2025, about half have "homeowners" or "HOA" in the name, and one in five "property owners" or "POA".

In a condo, an unpaid fee is a lien on the unit under state law. The association can foreclose in court, asking the court to sell the unit to pay the debt, as a lender can with a mortgage. For a house, the association's papers list what it can do. Can an HOA foreclose in South Carolina? covers the steps, and HOA violations and fines covers fines.

A name does not always tell you the kind of home. A few names mix a condo word with "property owners" or "homeowners". Some carry no label word at all, only a place name such as a Tower or Villas.

On this page, POA means a property owners association, not a power of attorney.

How is a condo association different from an HOA?

A condo association is an HOA too, but it runs a building, not a neighborhood. In a condo, the owners share the building. In a neighborhood of houses, you own your lot and the house on it. That one difference changes the fee, what it covers and how a lender looks at the home.

What do you own, and who fixes the roof?

In a condo you own your unit alone, plus a share of the shared parts, given as a percentage in the condo's founding papers.

Unless the founding papers say otherwise, the shared parts include the land under the building, the foundations, main walls, roofs, halls, lobbies, stairways, elevators and the central power and water systems. Every owner pays a share of their upkeep by that percentage, and the association insures the building. It may also enter your unit at reasonable hours to repair the shared parts, and for emergency repairs.

Who pays after a leak or a fire is on our who pays for condo damage page.

And in a neighborhood of houses?

South Carolina's HOA Act has no rule on who maintains or insures a house in a planned community. It is in the HOA's rules, in papers filed with the county. In most house listings, the fee does not include insurance.

What is a regime fee, and why is a condo fee higher?

A regime fee is the condo fee. South Carolina's condo law calls a condo a horizontal property regime. A condo fee is higher because it pays for the building: on the Grand Strand, the typical condo fee is nearly seven times the typical house fee.

In a year, that is about $7,800 for the typical condo and about $1,100 for the typical house, on top of the mortgage, the property tax and your own insurance.

What fees run here, by kind of home, and what each one covers is on our Myrtle Beach HOA fees page. The fee in each community is in our list of HOA fees by neighborhood.

About three in ten houses for sale have no association at all. Homes with no HOA has its own page.

Why do some homes pay two HOA fees?

Because some master-planned communities have two layers. A master association has rules over the whole community. Inside it, a neighborhood association or a condo association can have its own rules and its own fee. For you, that can mean two bills and two sets of rules. The name will not warn you: only 4 of the more than 500 associations in the lien count above have "master" in the name.

What do the two fees come to together?

The master's fee plus the fee of the association inside it. A listing's fee may leave out the second one, so we have no count of that total for homes here. For a home you send us, adding the two up is the second of our checks.

What surprises buyers in an HOA or a condo?

  1. 1
    Counting on the building's flood policy

    In a condo, the association insures the building, and a policy on your own unit is a separate one. Flood insurance has a limit: the building's National Flood Insurance Program policy and your own flood policy together pay at most $250,000 for your unit. Master insurance and your condo policy shows which policy covers what.

  2. 2
    Finding a second association after closing

    The seller's disclosure form has room for one association's charges and one contact. The Horry County Register of Deeds index is free to search online, but the county does not show the papers themselves to the public online. They are viewed and printed in Conway, or ordered by mail.

Does a condo association change your loan?

Yes, for many condos. A lender that follows Fannie Mae's rules reviews the whole condo project before a loan on an attached unit. That applies to a project of more than ten units, and to one of five to ten units inside a larger community or under a master association.

Three kinds of home skip the full review: a detached condo, a small condo project, and a house or townhouse in a neighborhood where every owner must belong to the HOA. A detached condo is a stand-alone home sold as a condo. Condos most lenders will not lend on covers buildings that fail it.

Does it matter if it is called a POA?

The real choice is between a condo and a house, not between an HOA and a POA:

A condo

  • +The association keeps up and insures the building, roof included.
  • +Your property tax bill is for your unit alone.
  • −The biggest monthly fee, because it pays for the building.
  • −Every owner shares the cost of big repairs to the building.

A house in an HOA or POA

  • +The smallest monthly fee.
  • +The lot and the house are yours alone.
  • −The policy on the house is a bill of its own.
  • −In some master-planned communities, a second association with its own rules and fee.

Common questions

HOA vs POA questions

Is an HOA the same as a POA in South Carolina?

Yes. Under the South Carolina Homeowners Association Act, the name does not matter. A group is an HOA when papers filed with the county make the owners pay it dues, except a timeshare plan.

Can a POA put a lien on my house?

Yes, if its papers filed with the county give it a lien for unpaid dues, as an HOA's papers can. That is a claim on your house for the unpaid dues. About one in five of the more than 500 associations that recorded a lien for unpaid charges in Horry County in 2025 had "property owners" or "POA" in the name.

Is a POA voluntary in South Carolina?

Only when no papers filed with the county make the owners pay it. A group with no such papers, such as a voluntary civic club, is not an HOA under South Carolina's HOA Act. If the papers on your home make you pay, the dues are not voluntary, whatever the group calls itself.

What is a regime fee in South Carolina?

The condo fee. South Carolina's condo law calls a condo a horizontal property regime, so the fee its owners pay is called a regime fee.

What do I own in a condo?

Your unit alone, plus a share of the shared parts, given as a percentage in the condo's founding papers. No owner can force the shared parts to be split up, and selling a unit sells its share with it.

Can a home have two HOAs?

Yes. A home in a master-planned community can belong to a master association and to a neighborhood or condo association inside it, each with its own papers and fee. Grande Dunes, Barefoot Resort and Prince Creek each have more than one association.
