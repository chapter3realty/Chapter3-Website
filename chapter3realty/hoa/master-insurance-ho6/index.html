Master policy and HO-6

Condo master insurance vs HO-6
Where the line falls, and who pays the deductible

By Timmy Fredrick Nash, Broker-in-Charge, 30+ years on the Grand Strand · Reviewed by Devin Day, Operations Officer · Updated July 28, 2026

WHERE THE LINE FALLS

The master deed decides where the association's coverage stops

A condominium building is covered by two insurance policies. The association buys a master policy, funded through your dues. You buy an HO-6, which is the standard name for a condominium unit owner policy. The first thing to establish is where one policy stops and the other starts.

South Carolina Code 27-31-240 says the council of co-owners, meaning all the unit owners acting together as the association, shall insure the property against risks. The Act defines property broadly at 27-31-20(k): the land, the building, all improvements and structures on the land, and the easements, rights and appurtenances belonging to it. So the statute covers the whole building. It does not say where the association's duty stops inside a unit.

That boundary is set in the recorded master deed. In South Carolina the master deed is the declaration, so the two words mean the same document here. Section 27-31-100 requires the master deed to describe each apartment, including its area and location, and to describe the general and limited common elements. That description, read next to the insurance article of the master deed and the master policy itself, is what tells you whether the association insures your cabinets, your flooring and the wiring behind your walls.

You will hear several labels for this: bare walls, walls-in, single entity, and all in. Treat them as industry shorthand. They are not defined in South Carolina law, and neither Fannie Mae nor Freddie Mac uses them. Both mortgage investors ask two narrower questions instead. Does the master policy cover the interior of the unit and the improvements to it. And does it carry a per unit deductible, meaning a deductible the master policy applies separately to each unit rather than once to the whole building. Ask those two questions, and take the answer from the documents rather than from anyone's summary of them.

This page is about condominiums, which South Carolina calls horizontal property regimes. Under 27-31-30 a regime exists only where the owner recorded a master deed submitting the property to it. If that was never recorded, the Horizontal Property Act does not apply and the recorded declaration alone defines what the association insures. The test is what was recorded, not what the building looks like from the street.

WHAT THE HO-6 COVERS

Your HO-6 has to cover whatever the master policy leaves out

An HO-6 is the condominium unit owner policy. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners describes the Condominium Unit Owners Form as insuring your personal property and your walls, floors and ceiling against the perils in what the industry calls the Broad Form, which is a standard named list of causes of loss. The South Carolina Department of Insurance describes an HO-6 as covering personal belongings, improvements made to the unit, and liability.

How much work that policy has to do depends entirely on the master policy. In a building where the master policy stops at the bare structure, your HO-6 has to pay to replace everything from there inward. In a building where the master policy covers the original interior finishes, your HO-6 covers much less. Same policy name, very different limit.

On a conventional loan sold to Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac, the lender checks this. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac changed these rules together on March 18, 2026. Fannie Mae Lender Letter LL-2026-03 and Freddie Mac Bulletin 2026-C were issued the same day and say substantially the same thing. Fannie Mae's letter replaced the older Selling Guide B7-3-04 requirements in their entirety, so a search that lands you on that Selling Guide page is showing you superseded text.

Under the current Fannie Mae rule, the borrower must have a unit owners property insurance policy when any portion of the interior of the unit or improvements to the unit are not covered by the master property insurance policy, or when the master property insurance policy includes a per unit deductible. The coverage must be at least the greater of two amounts: enough to cover the parts of the interior or improvements the master policy does not cover, so the unit can be restored to its condition before the loss, or the amount of the per unit deductible. Lenders had to apply this to all loan applications dated on or after July 1, 2026.

Freddie Mac says the same thing in its own words. For loan applications received on or after July 1, 2026, the lender, which Freddie Mac calls the Seller, must document that the borrower has an HO-6 policy when the master policy does not cover all or any portion of the interior of the unit or the improvements to the unit, or when the master policy includes a per unit deductible. The HO-6 limit must be at least the greater of the amount sufficient to repair the unit to its condition prior to the loss, or the amount of that per unit deductible. Separately, and effective with those March 2026 documents rather than on the July date, both Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac cap the HO-6 policy deductible at the greater of 5 percent of the coverage amount or $2,500.

These are the rules for a conventional loan that will be sold to Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac. FHA, VA and USDA loans have their own project and insurance rules, and a cash buyer has none of them. Ask your lender which set applies to you before you order the policy.

The practical consequence is simple. An HO-6 quoted on contents alone can fail a conventional lender's test in a building where the master policy stops at the structure. Get the master policy and the master deed early, so the policy is written once, at the right limit, instead of being rewritten a week before closing.

THE DEDUCTIBLE QUESTION

South Carolina law treats what the insurance does not pay as a common expense

Section 27-31-240 is one sentence long. It requires the council of co-owners to insure the property against risks, and it preserves each owner's right to insure his own apartment. It says nothing about the deductible, which is the part of a loss the policy does not pay.

The Act does deal with the shortfall, in a different section. Under 27-31-250(B), the cost of repair or replacement in excess of insurance proceeds and reserve must be considered a common expense. A deductible is, by definition, part of a repair cost that the insurance proceeds do not pay. Common expenses are shared by the owners pro rata under 27-31-190, meaning in the percentages computed under 27-31-60, which set each apartment's share by the value of that apartment rather than by a head count. Sums assessed but unpaid become a lien on the apartment under 27-31-210(a).

Two details are worth knowing. Section 27-31-250 also requires damaged property to be repaired or replaced promptly, unless repair is illegal under a state statute or local health ordinance, or eighty percent of the co-owners, including the owner of an apartment that is not to be rebuilt, vote not to rebuild. The bylaws may require a higher percentage than eighty. And the applicability note to the 2006 act that rewrote the section states that it applies to all horizontal properties governed by the Horizontal Property Act, notwithstanding a provision in the master deed or bylaws to the contrary.

A separate section, 27-31-260, covers property that is not insured, or where the insurance is not enough to cover reconstruction. It puts those rebuilding costs on the co-owners directly affected by the damage, in proportion to the value of their respective apartments, or as may be provided in the bylaws.

So the honest answer is this. No section of the South Carolina Horizontal Property Act uses the word deductible. The statute treats money that insurance does not pay as a common expense, and the detail is controlled by your building's master deed and bylaws. This is general information, not legal advice. Have a South Carolina real estate attorney read the insurance and assessment articles of the master deed on the specific building you are buying.

DEDUCTIBLE SIZE AND WIND

Conventional lender rules cap the per unit deductible, and wind carries its own

On the coast, check the deductible first. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners notes that in some locations there are catastrophe deductibles, which are expressed as a percentage instead of a dollar amount. Read the master policy declarations page, which is the summary page at the front of the policy, and look for a deductible that applies only to wind or named storm damage, separately from the deductible for other losses.

Mortgage rules now cap the per unit figure in a building that Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac will finance. Under Lender Letter LL-2026-03 and Bulletin 2026-C, the maximum allowable per unit deductible for all required property insurance perils covered by a master property insurance policy is $50,000 per unit, for loan applications dated on or after July 1, 2026. Freddie Mac Bulletin 2026-C also states that per occurrence deductibles for required perils may not exceed 5 percent of the limit maintained for building coverage.

There is a fix an association can buy. A deductible buy back policy is insurance the association buys to cover its own deductible, and Freddie Mac Bulletin 2026-C accepts one toward its deductible limits. If a building's deductible looks large, ask whether a buy back is in place.

Freddie Mac also states that named storms designated by the U.S. National Weather Service or NOAA by a name or number are a required component of windstorm coverage, on the borrower's own policy and on one to four unit property. That matters because it ties the trigger to a published, objective event rather than to a judgement call after the fact.

Wind coverage for Grand Strand condominium buildings has changed quickly. The South Carolina Department of Insurance reported that the South Carolina Wind and Hail Underwriting Association, the state wind pool that writes wind coverage on the coast when private carriers will not, began 2024 covering 70 condominium associations across 735 buildings, was down to 20 associations and 175 buildings at January 31, 2025, and reached seven associations and 23 buildings by August 1, 2025, unchanged at year end 2025. The department says those associations found better wind coverage at a lower price in the admitted market, meaning carriers licensed by the state. The same report states that condominium associations with wooden buildings over fifty years old and located close to the ocean have been experiencing significant challenges finding affordable coverage, and that combining the two wind pool zones, Horry County has the highest exposure of any county, at $2.35 billion of insured value on the books.

LOSS ASSESSMENT COVERAGE

Loss assessment coverage has no legal minimum, so check the number yourself

Loss assessment coverage is an endorsement, meaning an add-on to a policy, that responds when the association levies an assessment on the owners. It is the coverage on your own policy that answers a shortfall left by the association's policy. If a deductible or an uninsured shortfall becomes a common expense, you owe your share, and this is the coverage that may pay it.

Fannie Mae used to size this for you, and no longer does. Until March 2026, a master policy whose per unit deductibles added up to more than 5 percent of the coverage amount was allowed only if the borrower's own policy carried deductible assessment coverage and loss assessment coverage sufficient to cover assessments above that 5 percent, divided by the number of units. Lender Letter LL-2026-03 replaced that with a flat limit: a per unit deductible on a master policy may not exceed $50,000 per unit, and the unit owner policy must be written for at least the amount of that per unit deductible. Freddie Mac Bulletin 2026-C made the same change and retired the old 5 percent per unit calculation. Neither investor now publishes a loss assessment limit, so the number on your declarations page is not measured against any published standard.

South Carolina law is silent on this. Nothing in the Horizontal Property Act or the Homeowners Association Act requires loss assessment coverage, sets a minimum limit, or regulates whether a master policy deductible may be passed through to owners. The limit is whatever is printed on your own declarations page.

We do not print a typical default limit on this page. We could not verify one at a primary source, and a made up benchmark is worse than none. Use the arithmetic instead. The fewer units in the building, the larger the average owner's share of the same deductible. Your own share is not an even split. Under 27-31-190 and 27-31-60 it is set by the percentage assigned to your apartment in the master deed.

  • ✓Find the number. Read the loss assessment limit off your own HO-6 declarations page, as a dollar figure, not as a listed feature.
  • ✓Ask what triggers it. Get your agent to confirm in writing whether the endorsement responds to an assessment that arises from the master policy deductible.
  • ✓Compare it to the building. Put your limit next to the master policy's per unit deductible and next to its per occurrence deductible.
  • ✓Reprice it before you close. Raising this limit is a change to your own policy, not to the association's, so it is the one part of the picture you control.

BEFORE YOU OFFER

Six documents to read before you make an offer on a condo

This is a document review. Two documents and one endorsement decide most of it: the recorded master deed, the master policy, and your loss assessment endorsement. Here is the list we work through on a condominium purchase, before the offer where the seller will provide it, and during due diligence at the latest.

  • ✓The insurance article of the master deed. It defines what the association insures at the unit boundary, and it is a recorded document, so it can be pulled at the county recording office.
  • ✓The master policy declarations page. It shows the coverage amount, every deductible on the policy, and whether wind or named storm damage carries its own deductible.
  • ✓Any per unit deductible. If one exists, a conventional lender will require an HO-6 and will size it against that figure, so find it before you shop for insurance.
  • ✓Your own HO-6 declarations page. The loss assessment limit is printed on it, and it is the number most buyers never look at.
  • ✓The association's budget and reserve position. A deductible or an uninsured shortfall becomes a common expense, and reserves are the first place that money is supposed to come from.
  • ✓The statement of amounts due. Under S.C. Code 27-31-220 the council of co-owners must, on request, issue a purchaser a statement of the amounts the seller owes, and the purchaser's liability is limited to the amount in that statement.

Master Policy & HO-6 FAQ

What is the difference between condo master insurance and an HO-6?

The master policy is bought by the condominium association and paid for through your dues. It insures the building. An HO-6 is the policy you buy for your own unit. South Carolina Code 27-31-240 requires the council of co-owners, meaning all the unit owners acting together as the association, to insure the property against risks, without prejudice to the right of each co-owner to insure his apartment on his own account. Where one policy stops and the other starts is set by the recorded master deed and by the master policy itself, not by statute. Whatever the master policy does not cover inside your unit, your HO-6 has to cover.

Does the condo master policy cover the inside of my unit?

Sometimes. It depends on the recorded master deed and on the master policy, and the two have to be read together. Use Fannie Mae's test: does the master policy cover the whole interior of the unit and the improvements to it, and does it carry a per unit deductible. If any part of the interior or improvements is uncovered, or if there is a per unit deductible, Fannie Mae requires you to carry your own unit owner policy, written for at least enough to restore the unit to its condition before the loss, or the amount of that per unit deductible, whichever is greater. That is Lender Letter LL-2026-03, dated March 18, 2026, which replaced the older Selling Guide B7-3-04 requirements in their entirety.

Who pays the master policy deductible in a South Carolina condo?

No section of the South Carolina Horizontal Property Act uses the word deductible. Section 27-31-240 requires the council of co-owners to insure the property and stops there. Section 27-31-250(B) says the cost of repair or replacement in excess of insurance proceeds and reserve must be considered a common expense. Common expenses are shared by the owners pro rata under 27-31-190, in the percentages computed under 27-31-60, and sums assessed but unpaid become a lien on the apartment under 27-31-210(a). Your master deed and bylaws control the detail. This is general information and not legal advice, so have a South Carolina attorney read the insurance article of your building's master deed.

Do I need an HO-6 to get a mortgage on a Myrtle Beach condo?

On a conventional loan sold to Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac, yes, in two situations. You need one when any portion of the unit interior or the improvements to it is not covered by the master policy, and you also need one when the master policy carries a per unit deductible, even if the interior is fully covered. The limit must be at least the greater of the cost to restore the unit to its condition before the loss, or the amount of that per unit deductible. Fannie Mae Lender Letter LL-2026-03 and Freddie Mac Bulletin 2026-C, both dated March 18, 2026, set this for applications dated on or after July 1, 2026. FHA, VA and USDA loans have their own rules, and a cash buyer has none of them.

How much loss assessment coverage should I have on my HO-6?

There is no legal minimum. Nothing in South Carolina's Horizontal Property Act or Homeowners Association Act requires loss assessment coverage or sets a limit, so the number printed on your declarations page, the summary page at the front of your policy, is the number you have. Fannie Mae published a benchmark until March 2026 and then retired it. Lender Letter LL-2026-03 replaced the old 5 percent per unit calculation with a flat cap on the master policy instead: a per unit deductible may not exceed $50,000 per unit, and the unit owner policy must be written for at least that amount. Freddie Mac Bulletin 2026-C says the same. Compare your loss assessment limit to the building's per unit deductible and per occurrence deductible, and ask your agent in writing what triggers the endorsement.

Does a condo master policy have a separate hurricane deductible?

It can. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners notes that in some locations there are catastrophe deductibles, which are expressed as a percentage instead of a dollar amount. Read the master policy declarations page and look for a deductible that applies only to wind or named storm damage, separately from the deductible for other losses. On your own policy, S.C. Code 38-75-755(B)(1)(e) requires your insurer to disclose whether a separate deductible is required for hurricane, wind or named storm damage, and to include an example showing how the deductible functions for a policy valued at one hundred thousand dollars. Freddie Mac Bulletin 2026-C also requires named storm coverage, meaning storms the U.S. National Weather Service or NOAA designate by a name or number, as part of windstorm coverage on the borrower's own policy.
