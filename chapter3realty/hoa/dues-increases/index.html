Dues increases

How much an HOA can raise your dues
in South Carolina

By Timmy Fredrick Nash, Broker-in-Charge, 30+ years on the Grand Strand · Reviewed by Devin Day, Operations Officer · Updated July 28, 2026

South Carolina law does not cap how much an HOA can raise dues. No state statute sets a percentage limit or a dollar limit. The only state notice rule is S.C. Code 27-30-140, which requires an association to notify homeowners at least forty-eight hours in advance of the meeting in which a decision to raise the annual budget is made. That section also says it does not apply to an association incorporated under the South Carolina Nonprofit Corporation Act, so it does not reach every association. What limits the size of an increase is the recorded declaration and bylaws, not state law.

THE STATUTE

South Carolina law sets no cap on a dues increase, only a notice rule

The South Carolina Homeowners Association Act is Article 1 of Title 27, Chapter 30. It has seven sections: short title, definitions, enforceability and recording, annual budget increases and notice, application of access to documents provisions, jurisdiction of magistrates court, and construction of the article. None of them sets a maximum increase. There is no percentage ceiling, no dollar ceiling, and no formula tied to inflation.

The one section that mentions raising a budget is a notice section, not a limit. Section 27-30-140 requires an association to give homeowners notice "at least forty-eight hours in advance of the meeting in which a decision to raise the annual budget is made." Read that phrase closely. The notice runs to the meeting, not to the decision and not to the increase taking effect.

Forty-eight hours is two days. The same section lists how that notice may be given. Its words are: "Notice of the meeting may be through posting notice: (a) in a conspicuous place in a common area in the community; (b) on an Internet website maintained by the homeowners association; (c) by electronic mail; or (d) through methods provided in the association's bylaws that ensure actual notice." Read literally, a notice posted on a board in a common area two days before the meeting meets the statutory minimum.

You will find a claim online that South Carolina requires fourteen days notice before a dues increase. It does not. The enacted number is forty-eight hours. Longer figures come from proposed legislation that never became law. S.819 of the 2013 to 2014 session would have created a new Title 27, Chapter 52. It set a twenty-one day notice for an annual or regularly scheduled meeting and listed a budget change among the topics that notice had to cover. Its fourteen day notice was for a hearing on an alleged violation, not for a dues increase. The bill was prefiled December 10, 2013 and went to a Judiciary subcommittee on March 3, 2014, where it died. That chapter language has been filed in more than one session and has never been enacted. Title 27, Chapter 52 does not exist in the South Carolina Code.

So the state floor is thin, and it is the floor, not the rule. If you want more warning than two days, it has to come from the association's own documents.

THE CARVE OUT

The forty-eight hour rule does not apply to every association

Section 27-30-140 has a second subsection that most summaries leave out. It reads: "The provisions of this section do not apply to a homeowners association that is incorporated under the South Carolina Nonprofit Corporation Act found in Chapter 31, Title 33."

That is not a footnote. An association organised as a nonprofit corporation sits outside the section entirely, so the forty-eight hour notice does not apply to it by statute. Many South Carolina associations are incorporated that way. Whether a particular association is incorporated is a matter of public record, and the answer decides whether that rule reaches it at all. Ask the question before you rely on the number.

For an incorporated association, meeting notice comes from the Nonprofit Corporation Act and the bylaws. Section 33-31-705(a) says a corporation "shall give notice consistent with its bylaws of meetings of members in a fair and reasonable manner." Subsection (c) treats notice as fair and reasonable if members are told the place, date and time of each annual, regular and special meeting of members no fewer than ten days before the meeting date, or thirty days if notice is mailed by other than first class or registered mail, and no more than sixty days before it.

Read the scope carefully, because it is easy to overstate. Section 33-31-705 governs meetings of members. Whether your budget is adopted at a meeting of members or by the board is set by the declaration and bylaws, not by either statute. Answer that first, then you know which notice rule you are actually under.

WHAT ACTUALLY LIMITS IT

The recorded declaration is the only real limit on the size of an increase

Section 27-30-130(A) makes recording the condition of enforceability. Governing documents must be recorded in the clerk of court, Register of Mesne Conveyance, or register of deeds office in the county where the property is located. That has a useful consequence for a buyer: the documents that control a dues increase are public, and you can read them before you make an offer.

In a condominium, the payment obligation is statutory. Section 27-31-190 says co-owners "are bound to contribute pro rata in the percentages computed according to SECTION 27-31-60 toward the expenses of administration and of maintenance and repair of the general common elements." The same section adds that no co-owner may exempt himself by waiver of the use or enjoyment of the common elements. Section 27-31-160 requires the bylaws to state the manner of collecting from the co-owners for payment of the common expenses, and to state that "a majority of at least fifty-one percent is required to adopt decisions."

Outside a condominium, Chapter 30 gives you less than people expect. It creates no cap, no lien, no collection procedure and no vote threshold. Every one of those, if it exists in your community, exists because the recorded declaration created it. Here is what to look for when you read it.

If none of those clauses is in the declaration, the honest answer is that nothing in South Carolina law limits the size of the increase. That is not a comfortable answer, but it is the accurate one, and it is better to know before you buy than after.

  • ✓An annual cap. Some declarations state a maximum percentage the board may add without an owner vote. Check whether the cap runs off the prior year or off a fixed base year.
  • ✓A vote threshold. Check what share of owners must approve an increase above the cap, and whether that share is of all owners or only of those who vote.
  • ✓Carve-outs from the cap. Check whether insurance, utilities or taxes are excluded. An insurance exclusion makes a cap much weaker on the coast.
  • ✓Who adopts the budget. Check whether the board adopts it outright or the members ratify it, and what notice the bylaws require for that meeting.
  • ✓Declarant provisions. Check whether a class of membership votes differently while the developer still controls the association.

COASTAL COST PRESSURE

Property insurance is a documented driver of budget pressure on this coast

The South Carolina Department of Insurance publishes an annual status report on the coastal property market. The 2025 report, dated January 31, 2026, states: "At the same time in South Carolina, condominium associations with wooden buildings over fifty years old and located close to the ocean have been experiencing significant challenges finding affordable coverage." That is a department level statement about a category of building, and it names no association.

The same report shows condominium associations leaving the state wind pool quickly, and it is worth reading the direction correctly. The South Carolina Wind and Hail Underwriting Association covered 70 condo associations and 735 individual buildings at the start of 2024, 20 associations and 175 buildings at January 31, 2025, and seven associations and 23 buildings by August 1, 2025, unchanged at year end. The department's stated reason is not distress. Its words are: "These associations found better wind coverage at a lower price in the admitted marketplace." Scale still sits here, though. At December 31, 2025, Horry County had the highest wind pool exposure of any county in the state, $2.35 billion of in force insured limits, ahead of Charleston at $1.809 billion and Beaufort at $1.539 billion.

Loan rules push on the budget from the other side. Freddie Mac's Bulletin 2026-C, dated March 18, 2026, provides that if a master property insurance policy includes a per unit deductible, that deductible may not exceed $50,000 per unit, for applications received on or after July 1, 2026. The same bulletin raises the reserve allocation for capital expenditures and deferred maintenance from a minimum of 10 percent to a minimum of 15 percent of annual budgeted assessment income, for applications received on or after January 4, 2027.

An association that wants its units to stay financeable has to budget to those rules. Higher reserve funding and a compliant insurance program both land in the annual budget, and the annual budget is where your dues come from. We cannot tell you why any particular association raised its dues, and we will not speculate about one. We can tell you the two lines to read: the insurance renewal and the reserve line.

TWO DIFFERENT THINGS

A dues increase and a special assessment are not the same charge

A dues increase changes the recurring amount every owner pays on the regular schedule. It changes the budget itself, and it stays changed. A special assessment is a separate charge levied on top of dues, for a specific cost, usually once. They are decided under different provisions and they appear in different places in the paperwork.

Section 27-30-140 is about the annual budget. It says nothing about special assessments, and no other section of Article 1 sets a notice period for one. Whatever notice you get for an assessment comes from the declaration and bylaws.

The difference matters most at closing. In a condominium, Section 27-31-200 requires unpaid assessments to be paid out of the sale price or by the buyer, ahead of everything except past due taxes on the apartment and duly recorded mortgages and encumbrances. Section 27-31-220 makes the purchaser jointly and severally liable with the seller for amounts owing up to the time of conveyance, and requires the council of co-owners to issue a statement of those amounts to any purchaser "upon his request." The purchaser's liability is then limited to the amount set forth in that statement. Note the trigger. It is the purchaser's request. Nobody is required to volunteer it.

For the mechanics of one off charges, read our page on special assessments. For how reserve funding works and why lenders look at it, read our page on reserves.

BEFORE YOU BUY

Six documents show an increase coming before you are committed

The documents that would show an increase coming already exist before you go under contract. The only real question is whether you get them in time to act on them.

South Carolina's statutory access rights run to members, not to buyers, and they are narrower than people assume. Section 27-30-130(B) reaches only "Rules, regulations, and amendments to rules and regulations," and it does not apply at all where those rules are posted in a conspicuous place in a common area or available on an association website. Section 27-30-150 applies the Nonprofit Corporation Act inspection provisions to associations not subject to that Act, and only for the annual budget and the membership list. Neither provision reaches the declaration or the bylaws, and neither runs to a person under contract. A buyer's route is the purchase contract, the seller, and the county recording office, not a demand letter to the association.

The state's own complaint data says this is exactly where it breaks down. The South Carolina Department of Consumer Affairs 2026 HOA report, covering calendar year 2025, found that 32 percent of complainants received a copy of the governing documents prior to purchase, while 53 percent received them after. Horry County produced 31 percent of all HOA complaints in the state and led per capita at 0.38 per 1,000 residents. The top three concerns raised were failure to adhere to or enforce covenants and bylaws at 16.8 percent, maintenance and repairs at 11.7 percent, and requests to access information or view documents being ignored at 9.5 percent.

The South Carolina seller disclosure statement does have to cover whether the property is subject to governance of a homeowners association, under Section 27-50-40(A)(9). But Section 27-50-40(B) gives the owner the option to state that the owner is making no representations. Treat the disclosure as a prompt to go and check, not as an answer.

Put document delivery and a review period in the contract, in writing, with a date on it. That is the part of this a buyer actually controls.

  • ✓The current annual budget, plus the two prior years. A three year trend tells you more than any single year.
  • ✓The reserve line in the budget, and the reserve study if one has been done.
  • ✓Board and member meeting minutes for the last twelve months. Insurance renewals and large repair debates usually surface here first.
  • ✓The master insurance declarations page and the terms of the most recent renewal.
  • ✓The recorded declaration and bylaws, read specifically for a cap, a vote threshold and any carve-out.
  • ✓In a condominium, the statement of amounts due under Section 27-31-220. Ask for it in writing, because the statute only requires it on request.

Common questions

Dues Increases FAQ

How much notice does an HOA have to give before raising dues in South Carolina?

Section 27-30-140 requires notice at least forty-eight hours in advance of the meeting in which the decision to raise the annual budget is made. The notice runs to the meeting, not to the increase itself. It may be posted in a conspicuous place in a common area, posted on a website the association maintains, sent by electronic mail, or given by a bylaws method that ensures actual notice. The same section says it does not apply to an association incorporated under the South Carolina Nonprofit Corporation Act. For an incorporated association, the bylaws and Chapter 31 of Title 33 govern instead.

Is there a limit on how much an HOA can raise dues in SC?

No. Neither the Homeowners Association Act, at Title 27 Chapter 30, nor the Horizontal Property Act that governs condominiums, at Title 27 Chapter 31, sets a maximum dues increase. There is no percentage ceiling and no dollar ceiling in South Carolina law. Any limit that applies to your community sits in the recorded declaration and bylaws, so read them for a cap, for a vote threshold above that cap, and for any carve-out that excludes insurance or utilities from the cap.

Does South Carolina require 14 days notice before an HOA raises dues?

No. The forty-eight hour rule in S.C. Code 27-30-140 is the enacted figure. Longer periods appear only in bills that never became law. S.819 of the 2013 to 2014 session proposed a new Title 27, Chapter 52 with a twenty-one day meeting notice, and its fourteen day notice applied to a hearing on an alleged violation rather than to a dues increase. It died in a Judiciary subcommittee on March 3, 2014, and that chapter does not exist in the South Carolina Code. A current bill, H.3425, would add a forty-eight hour notice before an association spends funds not in the approved budget. It was referred to the House Committee on Labor, Commerce and Industry on January 14, 2025 and is still there.

Can an HOA raise dues without a vote of the owners?

South Carolina law does not require an owner vote to raise dues. No section of Title 27 Chapter 30 or Title 27 Chapter 31 conditions an increase on a member vote. Whether a vote is required, and at what threshold, comes from the recorded declaration and bylaws. In a condominium, Section 27-31-160 requires the bylaws to state that a majority of at least fifty-one percent is required to adopt decisions. Whether the budget is adopted at such a meeting or by the board is itself a question for the documents.

Why are HOA dues going up in Myrtle Beach?

We cannot tell you why any specific association raised its dues, and we will not guess at it. What is on the record is that property insurance is under pressure on this coast. The South Carolina Department of Insurance, in its coastal property report dated January 31, 2026, says condominium associations with wooden buildings over fifty years old and located close to the ocean have been experiencing significant challenges finding affordable coverage. At December 31, 2025, Horry County also had the highest wind pool exposure in the state, $2.35 billion of in force insured limits. Read the budget and the insurance renewal.

What happens if I do not pay a dues increase I disagree with?

In a condominium, Section 27-31-190 says no co-owner may exempt himself from contributing by waiving use or enjoyment of the common elements. Unpaid common expense assessments become a lien on the unit under Section 27-31-210(a), which may be foreclosed by suit in like manner as a mortgage of real property. Magistrates court has concurrent jurisdiction over monetary disputes within the Section 22-3-10 limit of $7,500, but under Section 22-3-20(2) it has no cognizance of a civil action when title to real property comes in question. This is general information, not legal advice. Talk to a South Carolina attorney.
