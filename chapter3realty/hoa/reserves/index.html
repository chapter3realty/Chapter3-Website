Can a Myrtle Beach condo association pay for its own roof?
Its reserve fund tells you. South Carolina law does not require one.

  • 56% of condosfor sale are in buildings 30+ years old
  • 20 yearsthe federal planning life of elevator machinery

By Devin Day, Operations Officer · Reviewed by Tim Nash, Broker-in-Charge · Updated November 12, 2026

A Myrtle Beach condo's roof and elevators wear out on a schedule. If its reserves cover the worn part, it can pay. If not, the owners do.

The short answer

Only if it has saved enough. Its reserve fund is its savings for big repairs. Compare the cash in that fund with what its reserve study, a report on the building's big shared parts, says the fund should hold by now. That share is called percent funded. The higher it is, the lower the chance the owners get a special assessment, a one-time bill to each owner for a cost the fund cannot cover.

What is an HOA reserve fund?

A reserve fund is money a homeowners association (HOA) or condo association saves each year for big repairs it knows are coming. The roof, the elevators and the paving are the usual ones.

  • Account 1The operating budgetPays this year's bills, from this year's dues.
  • Account 2The reserve fundSaves for the big repairs ahead: the roof, the elevators, the paving.
  • When the reserve is shortThe ownersPay the rest, often as a special assessment.

Saving ahead spreads the cost of a roof across every owner who uses it. Without savings, the whole bill falls on whoever owns a unit the year the roof fails.

A reserve study is a report on the building's big shared parts: what each one costs, how long it lasts, and how much the fund should hold by now.

Does South Carolina require HOA reserves or a reserve study?

No. South Carolina law does not require a condo or an HOA to keep a reserve fund, to put any amount into one, or to have a reserve study. How much a building saves is up to its board and its own documents.

In a South Carolina condo, every owner shares the cost of keeping up and repairing the shared parts. Each unit's share is the percentage set for it in the condo's founding papers, the recorded documents that created it. After damage, the bill is paid in this order:

  1. InsurancePays first, for what the master policy covers.
  2. The reserve fundPays next, from what the board has saved.
  3. The ownersShare the rest, by each unit's percentage in the founding papers.

A condo board must keep a book of every receipt and expense, with repair costs shown, open to every owner. A buyer is not yet an owner, so we get the papers through the seller.

How much should a Myrtle Beach condo association have in reserves?

Enough to cover the part of each big repair that has already worn out. A roof halfway through its life should have about half its cost saved.

In the weak band, repairs are often put off. In the strong band, the risk of a special assessment is low, and a fund does not need to be full to get there.

Among the firm's own clients, about a third were weak and a quarter strong. Those are associations that pay for a study, and the firm says they may be more careful with money than most.

Percent funded is a snapshot of today. It does not grade the plan, so we read the plan as well.

What do lenders require for condo reserves?

For most conventional condo loans, the lender tests the building's budget. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac buy these loans from lenders, and the test is theirs.

  • How a building passesAt least 10 percent of its dues goes into reserves. Or it saves what a current reserve study says it should.
  • When it failsA buyer there cannot get a conventional loan that follows these rules, though some other loans still work. When you sell a condo there, your buyer's lender runs the same test.

10 percent or 15 percent?

Freddie Mac raises its line to 15 percent for loan applications received on or after January 4, 2027. It says it made the change with its regulator and in alignment with Fannie Mae. Fannie Mae's own guide still says 10 percent.

From that date, a building that puts 12 percent into reserves fails Freddie Mac's test and passes Fannie Mae's guide as it reads now. Which one applies depends on which company buys your loan. Your loan officer can tell you before you apply.

Freddie Mac made the change after buildings with underfunded reserves turned out to be the same ones needing critical repairs. It says owners in those buildings can face special assessments or higher dues.

A building can pass and still be weak

The test reads one line of this year's budget, not the cash in the bank. Our example building puts 10.4 percent of its dues into reserves, so it passes while 26 percent funded. Try your own building's numbers:

Why do reserves matter more on the Grand Strand?

Most condos for sale here are in buildings 30 years or older, and the elevator and oceanfront buildings are older still.

HUD plans on elevator machinery lasting 20 years and a flat roof 15 to 20. A 30-year-old tower has passed one planning life of each.

The HOA fee will not tell you which condo buildings have saved. In buildings 30 years or older, the middle listed fee is about $700 a month, close to the $640 for all condos for sale. We read the study instead.

What do other states require for condo reserves?

Florida, New Jersey, Maryland and Virginia make condo boards study their reserves on a schedule. A buyer from one of them should not expect the same here.

What if a building has no reserve study, or an old one?

No law here requires a reserve study, so a building can have none, or one that is years old. A missing study is a reason to look harder, not proof of a problem.

Without one, we judge the building from its budget, its balance sheet, its board minutes and the age of its big parts.

When there is a study, a lender accepts it, or an update, only if it is no more than three years old. Among the same reserve-study firm's clients, associations that update at least every three years were told to charge a new special assessment about half as often as those that update every five.

What should a buyer check in a condo's reserves?

Our agents read the HOA documents for a buyer. For reserves, these five tell us whether the building can pay for its own roof:

  1. The reserve study. Its date, the percent funded and its plan. A plan that only keeps the fund above zero is a warning.
  2. The budget's reserve line. The share of the dues it puts into reserves.
  3. The balance sheet. The cash in reserves, against what the study says should be there.
  4. The age of the big parts. The roof, the elevators, the paint and the balconies, against HUD's lives and the year the building was built.
  5. The board's minutes. Repairs put off, or a special assessment under discussion. The HOA documents a buyer receives are only part of what we ask for.

When you see the papers, and when you can still back out

  1. Before you signIn most resales, the seller fills in a disclosure form. It asks whether the association has levied any special assessments.
  2. After you signThe standard South Carolina sales contract has a due diligence period. In it, you can end the contract with a written notice to the seller.
  3. Inside that periodRead the reserve study, the budget, the balance sheet and the minutes.

If they are weak, you can ask for a lower price or a credit at closing, or walk away.

Common questions

Reserve questions

Does South Carolina require a reserve study?

No, and it requires no reserve fund either. A board can still choose to get one, and a lender can accept a current study in place of its budget test.

Who pays a special assessment voted before closing?

The purchase contract decides, so it belongs in the offer. A bill the seller already owes is paid at closing, and a buyer can be held to it only up to the association's written statement of it.

Can I see an HOA's reserve records before I buy?

Members can. An HOA member can inspect and copy the budget and the accounting records after giving written notice five business days ahead. A buyer is not yet a member, so we ask the seller for them.

Does a newer condo building need strong reserves?

A newer building needs less saved by now. A building finished in 2015 is 11 years old, short of HUD's planning life for its elevator machinery and its flat roof, so less of each has worn out. Percent funded still applies: what the fund holds against that smaller amount.
