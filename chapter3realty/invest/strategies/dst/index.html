DST: the hands-off 1031 for a Grand Strand rental
Swap a Grand Strand rental for a share of a large property: no tenants, tax put off, about 6% to sales commissions.

  • What you getNo tenants, tax later
  • Who can buy$1M+ net worth or $200K+ income
  • Usual minimum$100,000
  • Sales commissionAbout 6% of your money

By Devin Day, Operations Officer · Reviewed by Tim Nash, Broker-in-Charge · Updated November 1, 2026

A Delaware statutory trust (DST) owns real estate and sells shares in it. A share can replace your rental in a 1031 exchange.

The short answer

In a DST, a sponsor, the real estate company that forms the trust, buys the property and runs it. You get a share of the cash it has left over. In return, you usually get your money back only when the trust sells, the payout can fall, and sales commissions come out of your money first.

It fits only if you are done managing, pass the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) wealth test, and will not need the money for years.

Should you sell your Grand Strand rental into a DST, another rental, or pay the tax?

On a $400,000 rental sale on the Grand Strand, the Myrtle Beach area, you choose between paying the tax now, keeping the work of a rental, or giving up control of the money. The numbers are our example, worked through below.

Which one fits

  • A DST, if you would rather own a share of a large property someone else runs than a rental of your own.
  • Another rental here, if you want to pick the property, set the rent and decide when to sell. Our 1031 exchange guide covers the deadlines and how we find a replacement on the Grand Strand.
  • Paying the tax, if you want the cash now, or the tax is small next to what a DST would cost you to enter.
  • Keeping the rental, if the work is the only problem. A property manager takes the calls, and our page on how long to hold a rental shows what waiting has meant here.

Why does the IRS allow a DST in a 1031 exchange, and who sells one?

The IRS, in Revenue Ruling 2004-86, has ruled that you may trade a rental for a share of a Delaware statutory trust with no tax on the gain yet. The trust must follow the ruling's limits, and the other 1031 rules must be met.

You buy the share through a securities firm or broker, called the DST broker on this page. Anyone who sells you a DST share must be registered with the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (FINRA), the SEC or the state before they can sell you anything. FINRA's BrokerCheck lists each securities firm and broker, with their licenses and record.

What do you give up in a DST, and can you lose money?

The ruling requires these limits on the trust:

  1. The trust cannot take in new money from anyone.A large repair has to be paid from the trust's own cash. That cash is not paid out to you.
  2. It cannot refinance its loan or sign a new lease.In the ruling, the trust may sign a new lease only if its tenant goes bankrupt or insolvent. A DST with many tenants, such as an apartment community, can lease the whole property to one master tenant. In one large sponsor's program, the master tenant is a company the sponsor owns. It signs the tenants, pays the costs of running the property and pays the trust its rent. Either way, the trust depends on one tenant's rent.
  3. You have no say in when the property sells.The trust's manager decides.

What does a DST pay you?

The trust pays out the cash the property has left, less reserves, on a set schedule. In the IRS ruling, that is every quarter. The amount moves with the rent and with the cash held back for repairs.

The SEC filings we checked have no payout figure. If the sponsor projects a payout, the projection is in its offering document, often called a private placement memorandum (PPM).

To compare a DST with your rental, set the PPM's projected payout for a year on your share next to what your rental leaves you in a year: the rent, less every cost you pay, the mortgage included. Ask the DST broker for the PPM before you name a DST in your exchange, and look for four things in it:

  • Every fee, with its amount.
  • The projected payout, and what it rests on.
  • How long the sponsor plans to hold the property.
  • Who the tenants are, and how long their leases run.

Can you get back less than you put in?

Yes. You get back your share of what is left of the sale price after the trust's loan is paid off. The sales commissions and the sponsor's fees were paid out of the money first, so the property has to gain value before your share is worth what you paid. If it sells for less than the trust paid, you get back less still.

Who can buy a DST?

Most DSTs are sold only to buyers who pass the SEC's wealth test, called accredited investors. For a person, you pass with either of these:

Net worth

Over $1 millionAlone or with a spouse or partner, not counting your home.

Income

Over $200,000Or $300,000 with a spouse or partner, in each of the last two years, with the same expected this year.

Holders of certain securities licenses also pass. Plan on needing to pass. Of the 160 DST real estate offerings we checked in the last year's SEC Form D filings, 159 had sold only to buyers who pass, as of the filing.

Most of them were filed under SEC Rule 506(c), part of the SEC's Regulation D. Under it, the sponsor must check that you pass. The rule lists ways to check:

  • Income: your IRS forms for the last two years, such as W-2s, 1099s, K-1s or your Form 1040.
  • Net worth: bank and brokerage statements from the last three months, and a credit report.
  • A letter from your CPA, your lawyer, a registered broker-dealer or an SEC-registered investment adviser that says they checked.

What does a DST cost, and what is the minimum investment?

The usual minimum is $100,000. Sales commissions are about 6 percent of the money, and the sponsor's other fees come on top. The Form D filings show only the sales commissions. The other fees are in the PPM.

What the commission means on a $400,000 sale

Our example is the same as on our capital gains page: a Grand Strand rental bought for $250,000 and sold for $400,000, with $60,000 of depreciation taken, a gain of $186,000. A 1031 exchange into a rental or a DST puts off all of the tax. A higher-income seller owes up to about $7,100 more than the chart shows, the 3.8 percent net investment income tax.

The sales commissions alone come to about half the tax the exchange puts off, or more, before the sponsor's other fees. You do not get the commissions back. The tax is still owed later, unless you exchange again or still own the share at your death.

How do you get your money out of a DST?

Mostly when the trust sells its property. The sponsor's planned hold is in the PPM. The SEC filings we checked do not show it. The trust in Revenue Ruling 2004-86 was set up to end after 10 years or when its building sold, whichever came first. A DST can end for you in four ways:

You want out early

Plan to stay in until the trust sells. DST shares cannot be resold on a public market unless the conditions of SEC Rule 144 are met. A private buyer, if you find one, decides what to pay.

The trust sells the property

You get your share of the money. Put it into a new 1031 exchange, with new deadlines, or pay the tax.

A REIT buys back your shares

Some DSTs are sold by large real estate companies called REITs (real estate investment trusts). After two years, the REIT may take back your shares, for cash or for units that cannot go into a later 1031 exchange, at the REIT's choice. If you get the units, your exchanges stop there. At least 13 REITs described this kind of buy-back in their latest annual reports.

You still own it at your death

Your heirs generally take it at its value on the date of death, so the gain you put off is not taxed to them. A rental you keep until death gets the same rule, 26 U.S.C. 1014.

Can a DST rescue a 1031 exchange that is going wrong?

Only if the DST is on the list you name by day 45. A 1031 exchange trades real property only for other real property. Every replacement, a DST share included, must be named in writing within 45 days of the sale. A DST named as a backup can still be bought if the rental you wanted falls through.

The exchange covers less of the tax if you keep cash or the sale pays off a mortgage:

  • Cash you keep. You can put part of the money into the exchange and keep the rest. The cash you keep is taxed, up to the amount of your gain.
  • A mortgage the sale paid off. It counts as cash you received and is taxed the same way, unless the replacement carries as much debt or you add cash. A DST can help: your share of its loan can count toward that debt. If your rental has a mortgage, raise it with your intermediary and tax adviser before you choose a DST. We give them the closing figures.

What does Chapter3 do when you sell a Grand Strand rental for a DST?

Chapter3 sells the rental. DST shares are securities, and Chapter3 does not sell them or advise on them. The DST broker sells the share. Each step of the sale and the exchange, with what our agent does:

  1. Before you list You choose a qualified intermediary, the company that will hold the money during the exchange, and start talking to a DST broker. After closing you have only 45 days to name the DST. We price the rental and set the listing date so the closing falls where your plan needs it. If a tenant is in the house, the time left on the lease changes which buyers can take it.
  2. The sale closes The money goes to your intermediary, not to you. Your 45 and 180 days start that day. We choose a buyer who can close on the date, and give your intermediary and closing attorney the closing figures.
  3. By day 45 You name the DST in writing, and any backups. If you want a rental here as a backup, we find and show you choices before day 45.
  4. By day 180 Your share of the trust is bought. If your tax return for the year of the sale is due before day 180, that due date comes first; an extension on the return moves it later. If the backup rental here is the one you buy, we handle the purchase so it closes in time.
  5. The trust sells You exchange again, or pay the tax. If the next exchange is a rental on the Grand Strand, we find it.

Common questions

DST questions

Is a DST the same as a 1031 exchange?

No. A 1031 exchange is the tax rule. A DST share is one kind of property you can buy with it.

Is a DST a REIT?

No. A REIT is a company, and its owners hold shares in it. A DST share counts, for federal income tax, as owning part of the trust's real estate itself. Because of that, it can go into a 1031 exchange.

How is income from a DST taxed?

For federal income tax, you are treated as owning part of the trust's real estate. Your share of the rent goes on your own return, and you deduct your share of the depreciation.

Are you personally liable for a DST's loan?

Not in the trust the IRS ruling describes. The trust took over the property's loan, and neither the trust nor any owner is personally liable on it.
